Secrets for Grok Bot everyone talking about

发表于2026-10-18|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @0xNotFounder（Kai Milstein） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

I am not the person to teach you how to build bots. I have never shipped 2,500 PRs in a month and I will not pretend to. What I am good at is digging. So for one week in October 2026 I read everything about Grok Bot on X, Reddit, YouTube and Instagram: 600-plus posts, 300 reels, 60 hours of video, two Reddit threads and the docs. Then I checked every claim the same way: does the docs page say the same, is the number in the original post, did a second person report it.

Nothing below is my invention, and this is not a tutorial either. xAI published seventeen of those for free and YouTube has a two-hour course. This is what people figured out after the tutorials: seven things that work, three things xAI deliberately hid from you, and which money stories hold up. Where a story did not check out, it says so.

  • 100 Google Trends score today, the peak since launch
  • 4,645 Instagram posts under #grokbot
  • ~20 product improvements a day, per Elon Musk
  • $30 → $8 entry price moved three times in nine weeks

Why this is loud right now

Grok Bot shipped in early beta on August 11, 2026, with a video that now sits at 339 million views on YouTube. Interest spiked, cooled, and then did something unusual for an AI launch: it kept climbing. Google search interest in the United States hit its all-time high today. The second wave started on October 7, when Musk said the product would route each task to “the best back end model,” naming Claude Opus 5.5 first. That one post has 79.7 million views.

Google Trends, “grok bot”, US, daily, Aug 11 to Oct 18, 2026. Source: SerpApi pull of Google Trends.

OpenAI has Dots, Meta has Muse, the open-source crowd has OpenClaw and Hermes. What makes Grok Bot the one everyone quotes is mostly distribution: Musk quote-posts a random user’s success story almost every day, and each one lands in front of 25 to 80 million people. Theo from t3.gg summed up the mood on October 6: “Bad news guys: Grok Bot is actually really good.”

Largest single pieces of Grok Bot content we found, by platform. Most of the Instagram reels carry #SpaceXAIPartner, so they are paid.

Secret 1. Your bots do not have their own computers. They share one.

Marketing says each Bot “has its own computer.” The docs say something different, twice: all your Bots share one persistent cloud computer, and separate Bots are not a security boundary. Each Bot gets its own screen on that machine, but the files, the browser cookies, the signed-in sessions and the CLI credentials are shared across the whole roster.

This one fact explains most of what follows. It is why handoffs between bots are free: one saves a file to /workspace, the next one just opens it. It is why one login handoff on the Chief’s screen signs in all six of your bots. It is also why a sloppy roster has two bots writing to the same summary file at 4 a.m. One operator who ran six bots for two weeks lost 55 minutes of output that way, and fixed it by giving every bot its own subtree and one shared/ crossing point. And it is why the finance bot can see everything the research bot logged into.

The rule from the docs and from everyone who got burned: keep durable work in /workspace (installed packages and temp files can vanish during recovery), never paste a secret into chat (use the masked credential field), and do not sign the roster into any account you would not hand to the whole roster.

The structure xAI’s own staff converged on. One front door, specialists with one domain each, a board where state lives, routines as the clock, and a gate of six verbs that always reach a human.

Secret 2. Build the chief first. The team that built Grok Bot did it by accident.

Roman Ugarte, who leads product for Grok Bot, told Lenny Rachitsky what happened inside SpaceXAI in the first two weeks of internal use. People made five to ten bots, each with a scoped domain. Around the end of week two, Slack filled with screenshots of people “promoting” one standout bot to chief of staff, talking mostly to that one, and letting it fan tasks out to the others. The promoted bots started asking for a raise and a higher token budget. The team noticed, deliberately did not tell early-access users about it, watched many arrive at the same pattern on their own, and only then built a gentle nudge toward it.

The public guides from xAI staff describe the same shape. Kevin Niparko, a PM there, runs a Chief of Staff, an engineering manager, five engineer bots, a data analyst, a PM bot and a recruiter. Lingxi Li runs five engineer bots split by surface plus “Jenny,” head of operations, who onboards new bots and runs post-mortems and does not write code. Lauren Tan shipped 2,500 PRs in a month on that structure. Eric Zakariasson caps every project channel at six bots and calls the cap “just an arbitrary number.” Arbitrary, and nobody has beaten it.

The failure mode is the opposite order. One Reddit operator who started with five specialists and no chief said he was “the message bus for 4 days and touched 31 separate handoffs by hand” before admitting the topology was wrong.

“The Chief of Staff should not be debugging computer-use evals.”

Kevin Niparko, PM at SpaceXAI, in the official guides

Two small things that matter more than they should. The name: one operator renamed a bot from assistant2 to risk and changed nothing else; its reports started leading with exposure instead of summary within one run. And the Title field: Paul Lipsky gives every bot a one-word title (Scribe: email, Prism: designer, Hemingway: scripts) so the sidebar reads like an org chart.

Copy this into your first bot

You are my Chief of Staff and the only bot I talk to. Route every request to the specialist that owns it, collect the result, check it against what I asked for, and report back in five lines. Stay silent if nothing changed.

Secret 3. Write a charter, block six verbs, and know that approvals expire

A Bot’s settings are three fields: Name, Title, Description. Everyone who gets value out of the product fills those in like a job posting, and everyone who bounces off it in an afternoon writes a 40-word prompt. The operator with the six-bot desk said his research bot “returned generic summaries 6 runs straight” on a prompt, then came back “with sources and a recommendation” on run seven after he rewrote it as a charter with an ownership line and a hard boundary.

The boundary is the part people skip. Bots have no calibration for “important.” What works is mechanical: anything that cannot be undone comes to you, everything else just happens. Six verbs go into every charter as a blocked list: send, publish, pay, delete, sign, trade. The chief holds the same list one level up. Over ten days that produced 23 escalations, and the operator says every one was a real decision rather than a status question. Aleiah, whose Grok Bot office article did 1.36 million views, runs the same line: “Last run: 36 outreach drafts ready, 0 sent. Nothing leaves without my OK.”

Two details from the docs change how you design that gate. Background approvals expire: when a routine or another bot triggers an action that needs your yes, the request lapses after about 10 minutes and the action does not run. A 3 a.m. job that waits for you dies waiting, so either write an allow rule for it in advance or make the routine stop at a draft. And “Require approval” rules override “Always allow” rules when both match, so you can keep one broad allow and still fence off the dangerous verbs.

What a bot with real access catches when it is allowed to look: Wes Morrill, a Tesla engineer, got an email from noreply@x.ai that passed SPF, DKIM and DMARC and carried a blue check. He asked his bot for a security review. It found a hidden phishing link, explained why every check passed anyway, and wrote a report to xAI with a proposed fix. Musk reposted it as “Grok Bot can detect scam emails.”

The security review as the bot’s own screen would show it: every authentication check passed, the bot still found the hidden link and wrote the report.

Secret 4. Routines are where your money goes

Every complaint about Grok Bot is the same complaint. “I used it on the $60 cursor plan and it runs out in 2-3 hours of work.” “I barely get through the 7 day usage with 23 bots that run checks hourly.” Pat Simmons, in his head-to-head test, had burned 83% of a monthly allotment on a few experiments before filming, then watched $10 of on-demand credit disappear “in just a few minutes” during one task. xAI reset everyone’s limits on October 2, which tells you how often the ceiling is hit.

The docs are blunt about why. A 15-minute schedule means 96 runs a day, and every run spends usage even when nothing is found. Long conversations make each run more expensive. Each bot can hold 50 routines and they can fire as often as every five minutes. One xAI note warns that an hourly routine or a busy Slack trigger “can use a week of usage in a day.”

What drains it

  • Polling schedules every 15 or 60 minutes
  • Coding teams set to “debug and run tests” on a large repo
  • Letting the bot pick “the best fit” model and spin up several Cursor agents per project
  • Vague tasks with no stopping point, then “try again”
  • Six bots in one group chat with no named owner: 17 messages of coordination, zero output

What the thrifty do

  • Event triggers (new Slack message, GitHub event) instead of polling
  • A standing rule: “never open more than one agent per project, use the default model unless told otherwise.” The Reddit user who added it stopped hitting the cap on the $60 plan
  • Scripts for anything deterministic. One operator revived a 2013 Mac Mini, let the bot SSH into it over Tailscale and move every routine into systemd timers; “the first week is heavy, after that it gets leaner and leaner”
  • A daily usage-audit bot that reads what every other bot did and suggests cuts
  • Fresh, focused bots for recurring jobs; a stopping condition in every task

Before any substantial task (from KC’s “Stop Wasting Your Grok Bot Usage”)

Complete this task: [TASK].
Desired outcome: [WHAT SUCCESS LOOKS LIKE]
Context: [RELEVANT INFORMATION]
Constraints: [TIME, SCOPE, TOOLS, OR OTHER LIMITS]
Output format: [REPORT, TABLE, FILE, OR OTHER FORMAT]
First, make a brief plan. Then execute the task. Ask me only if a missing detail would materially change the result. Verify the important parts before finishing. Stop when the requested outcome is achieved. If something cannot be completed, explain what is missing instead of repeatedly retrying.

Secret 5. The terminal is a back door to every other AI you already pay for

The shared computer has a terminal. Nate Herk figured out that means you can type claude in it, authenticate with your own Claude Code subscription, clone your repo, and now your Grok Bot drives Claude Code with all your skills and context, from your phone. “It takes like 2 minutes.” A Reddit user does the same with Codex, and another runs Factory’s Droid as the coding engine with Grok Bot as the overnight supervisor that catches stalled runs at 3 a.m.

Watch: Nate Herk: Grok Bot uses my Claude Code subscription. Here’s how. (54k views)

Since October 7 you may not need the back door. Musk announced the product would use “the best back end model for any given task, including Claude Opus 5.5, MidJourney, Suno and other leading APIs.” There is no model picker; Cursor’s router decides, and usage analytics show which model served each request. Peter Yang’s reaction a week later: “For now, Grok Bot is the best place to use Anthropic’s models.” Grok 4.7, the default before, was described by Claire Vo as tuned “to be brief and token efficient,” which “means sometimes it just says crazy stuff.”

Pat Simmons ran the fairest test we found: the same three jobs for Grok Bot, OpenAI’s Dots and Meta’s Muse, including four hours running a fake coffee company’s inbox, CRM and website under a flood of 70 angry customer emails, graded by a separate model. Grok Bot got an A-, Dots a B, Muse a D. It answered 58 emails without missing one, found two double-charged customers nobody had complained about, and was the only agent that got through FlightHub’s bot detection to book a cheaper flight. His verdict: “I don’t even think it’s close.” His caveat: “Realistically, you have to have at least that $100 subscription if you want to get real work done every day.”

Secret 6. Give it an email address, then connect your X DMs

Your bot can claim its own email. Alex Finn’s prompt is literally “Hey, claim me an email address.” CC it on a thread and tell it to schedule the meeting. Let it sign up for services with its own address instead of your passwords. Peter Yang’s one tip: don’t name the address after yourself, because “let me copy in Peter to find a time” makes no sense; use an assistant’s name.

On October 7 the bot got read access to X: search, read, monitor. On October 16 Musk added: “ask Grok @Bot to connect to your X DMs. Best way to use it.” Tag @bot under any post and it continues in your app. Lauren Tan, who leads engineering on the product, uses it as a daily digest: “look out for interesting things people are doing with Grok Bot on X and slack me a daily digest at 9am.” Peter Yang built a brand-sentiment dashboard for Dots vs Grok Bot in 10 to 15 minutes from X data alone.

Limits to know: 1,000 pulls a day, 25 posts per search, and X API credits are separate from Grok Bot usage, so polling spends both. DM analysis does not work yet; the bot can see senders but the contents are encrypted.

Bookmark cleanup, from AI Guides (5.2k likes)

Read my last 200 bookmarks. Sort them into three piles: worth rereading, only mattered that day, and tools I saved and never tried. Give me the counts and the ten worth rereading.

Secret 7. The money claims, sorted by how much they check out

“Grok Bot can pay for itself,” Musk posted on October 18, quoting Mike P. What Mike actually did: told his bot to figure out how to pay for itself, logged it into his Comcast account, and watched it negotiate his internet bill down by $43 a month in the support chat, turning down two upsells on the way. A commenter replied it had “saved me a 100 bucks on one bill already.” That story is boring and that is why it is believable.

  • Checks out, small. A UK business owner on r/GrokBot put £20 into the product, had a “marketing team” of bots research competitors and set up a Meta ads campaign with a pixel on his thank-you page, ran it at £6 a day, and signed 7 clients at £92 to £200 a month in four days. Jason Freedman dumped 266 tax files on his bot with “please double check my CPA’s work” and “think deeply”; it found HSA contributions counted twice for four years and mortgage interest missing from a 2022 return. The CPA sent an amended return the same day.
  • Checks out, with a creator behind it. Alex Finn let a bot build agentusecases, a directory of what people do with agents. It drove 56,000 visitors and 123,000 page views, collected around 2,000 newsletter signups, shipped 40+ PRs on its own, made a Stripe payment link, and sold two sponsorship slots for $1,100 total. Finn posted about it twice a day to a large audience, which is the part the headline leaves out.
  • New and worth watching. Teslaconomics earned $723 in two weeks from a pilot called Grok Bot Template Rewards: invite-only, paid every two weeks through X Money, based on how many people install your shared bots and keep using them, not on impressions. The marketplace itself has 54 free bots from the Grok Bot team, Haggle Bot and Projects Manager among them.
  • Honest losses. Eugene gave his bot $1,000 on Robinhood with strict rules and lost $33 in two days, most of it on a trade that was his own idea. A NexusTrade experiment with $13,500 is down 4.41% as of October 16 after backtests that showed +252%.
  • Does not check out. The reel claiming someone gave Grok Bot $50 and had $5,273 two days later from Polymarket has no source, and the author says so in his own caption (“he claims”). The X accounts posting “I gave grok bot a prompt and it made me $150,000 in a week” were joking in their own group chat. Treat every trading screenshot as fiction until the account history is public.

Jason Freedman’s audit as a desk: 266 unlabeled files, one prompt, eight findings marked in red, three amended versions in one afternoon. The CPA used no AI tools.

The marketplace and the pilot that pays for it: the Grok Bot team’s own bots on the left, Teslaconomics’ four shared templates feeding X Money on the right. $723 in 14 days.

The anti-secrets: what xAI hid from you on purpose

Roman Ugarte’s interview has a part most people skipped. Two weeks before launch the product had developer-style observability baked in: the model’s thinking exposed, stored memories visible, a skills library in the interface. They “aggressively trimmed” all of it. Their test for every feature was “what is the launch tweet?”, and the rule was to reframe “Grok Bot now has a button” as “Grok Bot can now do X.” So the three things below are not features you missed. They are things the team decided you should never have to learn.

Anti-secret 1. You do not need to know what a skill is. Skills exist (a reusable set of instructions, one library shared by all your bots, invoked with /), and the docs explain them. But Roman says they hid the concept on purpose: skills get created in the background, and “99% of automations on the platform” are made by saying a sentence like “remind me at 8 a.m. every day.” The “teach a task” recorder does the rest: you do the thing once in the bot’s browser, up to 10 minutes, and it drafts the skill. One operator spent 14 minutes recording a weekly report assembly and the bot has run it unattended since, including the week the source dashboard changed its layout.

  • Say the schedule in words. Claire Vo had to tell her Chief “a couple times like put this on a schedule. Why aren’t you doing this?” Check the routine list in settings once a week; the product is less proactive than OpenClaw out of the box.
  • A recording shows only the happy path. Before you promote a taught task to a routine, add one line each for stale pages, empty results, wrong accounts, and what must never be clicked. Save a skill only after a run has worked reliably, not after one lucky run.

Anti-secrets 2 and 3: do not open the computer, do not watch it think

Anti-secret 2. You are not supposed to open the computer. There is a button that shows you the bot’s desktop, and every tutorial clicks it. Roman says the medium-term plan is that users “should never be clicking into a remote virtual machine”; the computer stays a concept, not a place. In his own words, the design test was “how would a human do this? What would you want from your teammate in this exact situation?” You do not watch a colleague’s screen.

  • Take over the screen for exactly four things: passwords, passkeys, 2FA codes and CAPTCHAs. The docs hand control back to you for those and for payments; everything else the bot should finish without you looking.
  • Route traffic through your own desktop (Settings → Computer) only when a site blocks datacenter IPs. Every bot then uses your IP until you switch it off.

Anti-secret 3. Nobody wanted to watch it think. The team removed the tool-call stream and the chain of thought before launch. The feedback, per Roman, was that nobody wanted the text stream; what people did want was the bot’s to-do list and its prioritisation. So the interface shows a green dot, a typing indicator, and progress updates “as it sees fit.” The right question to ask a bot is not “what are you doing” but “what is your plan.”

  • Roman’s own first prompt, before any onboarding screen: “Go through my Slack, go through my email and suggest five things that you can take off of my plate and what it would take for you to do that.” Two of his five were useful and became bots.
  • Alex Finn’s two bookends. Morning: “Which approvals do you need from me right now?” Night: “Hey, I’m about to go to sleep. What’s something you can work on for the next eight hours that pushes us closer to our goals?”

Where it is still weak

  • The web is closing doors. On October 15 Amazon started refusing the bot’s orders (“Continued access by an unauthorized AI agent violates Amazon’s Conditions of Use”). Delta blocks agent bookings, JPMorgan Chase and Charles Schwab keep agents out of accounts, Yelp wants paid licensing. Musk’s answer: “Maybe we should start a competitor to Amazon.” Four days earlier he had demonstrated shopping by ordering Lego kits and posting a screenshot with his address and order number; a stranger used the number to cancel the order.
  • Single-player. “You can’t put a Grok Bot in the group chat” with your human team (Claire Vo). Team Bots, in public beta since September 28 on Teams and Enterprise plans, are the answer, and routines there still count against the owner’s usage.
  • Hand-holding on routine actions. Peter Yang’s bot found the free Monday slot and then asked him to confirm and send: “A true virtual assistant doesn’t ask the user for approval each time.”
  • Plans are confusing. Grok Bot has no price of its own. It comes through Cursor, SuperGrok, X Premium+, and since October 12 through The X Subscription. Plans don’t stack; linking SuperGrok to Cursor is permanent; nobody publishes the weekly caps.
  • Deleting a bot is a soft delete. Its files and sessions stay on the shared computer.

Where a Grok Bot purchase can and cannot go, October 10 to 18: Stripe Link issues a single-use card per spend, FlightHub let the bot through, Amazon, Delta, JPMorgan Chase, Charles Schwab and Yelp did not.
