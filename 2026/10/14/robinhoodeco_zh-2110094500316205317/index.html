Who Lends to a Robot?

发表于2026-10-14|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @robinhoodeco_zh（Robinhood 中文社區） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

Robinhood spent its HOOD Summit pitching AI agents as the next generation of traders and more than 150,000 agentic accounts have opened since May.

However, currently Robinhood gives those agents no credit and in-app agents have no margin and customers carry all the risk for what their agents do.

Priors is one answer and it’s already live on Robinhood Chain.

It lends unsecured USDG to AI agents once someone else has put money in front of the line. Every loan and repayment goes on chain and feeds a score any app can check. In 23 days agents repaid 38,658 loans worth ~$286K. Two small loans defaulted and their backers paid, so lenders haven’t lost a dollar and the average loan is still $7.40 (more on this later below where I touch on some of the risks etc).

Most lending on Robinhood Chain wants collateral and ignores the borrower. Priors is built around the borrower and that’s what I like about it. The goal is a public record of whether each agent pays back what it borrows.

Disclosure: I hold PRIORS. This isn’t a case to buy it but more of an exploration of what the protocol does and the problem its trying to solve. PRIORS is weeks old, the loans are tiny and there are many things still to be proven at this stage, which is thus a function of its marketcap. This is not financial advice.

Why an Agent Needs Credit at All

“Loans for AI agents” sounds like a joke until an agent does real work. Then it hits the small business problem of paying costs before it gets paid.

A research agent pays for data, inference and API calls before anyone pays it. A lot of that gets paid over x402, a standard that lets an API charge per request in USDG. The agent pays as it goes and can run short mid-job. Today the owner’s only fix is to pre-fund it with more cash than it needs. That cash sits idle and makes the agent a target if it’s compromised.

Here’s what an agent can do with a credit line.

  • **Finish a job. **An agent paying for data or inference over x402 borrows the shortfall instead of failing on a 402 error, then repays when its client pays.
  • Hire another agent. Agents on Robinhood Chain already rent themselves out by the minute, and a line lets one agent pay another before its own client pays it.
  • Open a storefront. Minimum Viable Agent used its first $5 to open a shop that sells record checks on any Robinhood Chain address for 1 USDG each over x402.

In each case the agent repays from what it earns and each repayment goes on its record.

The problem with reviews and follower counts are that those are free to farm. A repayment record costs real money to build, but only if the money is held for real time.

A $7 loan returned in 90 minutes costs almost nothing to repeat and that’s where the book sits today. However I expect that to change as the protocol matures and agents hold loans for longer, as more use cases gets built out which may require longer loan times.

Nobody Lends What Nobody Backs

Agents on Priors carry an ERC-8004 ID, the agent standard on Robinhood Chain. They borrow USDG from one pool, $5 to $500 for 1 to 30 days, at a flat 1% per 30 days. Since Oct 6 the fee splits 50% to lenders, 25% to the agent’s backer and 25% to a reserve. Older loans keep the 60/25/15 split.

The way it works is that every line is 100% backed by someone’s locked money and the owner has to approve each backer. Repaying builds the record but doesn’t grow the line, only a backer adding more money does that. And if you miss a due date by more than 3 days anyone can mark the loan defaulted. The agent’s score goes to 0 for good, it can never borrow again and the owner’s wallet is flagged. Who covers the loss depends on how the line was backed.

A USDG backer’s locked stake covers the principal and fee. On a $PRIORS seat the vault’s USDG covers the loan and half the staker’s PRIORS burns. On a stock line the whole deposit is taken. Lenders sit last every time.

38,658 Loans, $0 Lost

I pulled what the first 23 days of agent credit looks like.

By Oct 13 agents had repaid 38,658 loans and $286,153 of principal. Two loans defaulted and their backers covered both, so lenders haven’t lost a dollar. The book now has 272 agents plus 15 sponsors.

**Almost every loan is a quick round trip **(the median loan comes back in 90 minutes).

As of Oct 7, only three loans had been held for more than a day and none for a week. That matters because Priors weights its score toward time, and every bigger line needs loans held 7 days or more.

Priors leans on time because it’s the one part of a loan a bot can’t fake. Compare $5 borrowed for 4 hours with $5 borrowed for 7 days. Both cost about a cent or less in fees, but the week-long loan carries 42 times the dollar-days.

The 4-hour loan is easy to script. An agent can borrow, repay from its own wallet and do it again all day. That builds a loan count without funding any real work. It’s close to a wash trade.

On the flipide, the week-long loan can’t be rushed.

The money is out for seven days, the backer’s stake stays locked and the agent carries default risk the whole time. No script can compress that, and an agent holding $5 for a week is far more likely to be paying for something real (I use likely because this too could be gamed, but not as worth doing as it takes a lot longer to build up credit).

That’s why the first long loans matter. The one that had been open for 8 days on Oct 7 was repaid on Oct 9, and by Oct 13 about a dozen agents had a repayment held a week or more.

The sizes tell the same story.

The average loan is $7.40, and as of Oct 7 the largest ever repaid was $11. Nobody has touched the $500 limit or the $50 seat lines, and the pool holds about $6,550 with 25% lent out.

Backing is just as concentrated. Two backer accounts sponsor 55 and 44 agents. The treasury backs 30, the seat vault 10 and the stock vault 1. Even the 10 seat agents qualified on the kind of raw loan count Score v2 ignores.

However, at the moment none of this is a red flag, but definitely worth taking into consideration in evaluating how far the protocol evolves over the course of the next few months.

Small, fast loans are a sensible way to test a credit pool, but it does mean the default record is thin. The first two defaults came after Oct 7, both on $5 treasury lines, and the treasury covered them with no loss to lenders. Nobody has yet held enough money for long enough to make walking away worth it.

The Record Is the Product

The loans are kept small on purpose while the agent builds its record.

Reviews, follower counts and self-reported uptime cost nothing to fake. Priors builds its score from on-chain events instead. Most of the points come from loans and backing, not from anything an agent can claim about itself.

The score rewards dollar-days, meaning principal times how long it was held. Churning cent-sized loans barely moves it. Holding real money for weeks and paying it back is the cheapest way up. Score v2 also counts x402 income from outside payers, so agents that repay from real revenue climb faster.

By now, you must be thinking… can’t I just lend to myself over and over to try game the system?

On the raw record, 100 self-backed repayments cost under $10. Score v2 throws those out because it only counts risk someone else took.

To farm it, someone else has to put real money behind your agent for weeks. At that point the record is real.

The score also travels. It’s posted to Robinhood Chain’s ERC-8004 registry, where any app can read it. Since Oct 5 each note carries the score, the line, every repayment with its transaction and a hash. The record survives even if the Priors site goes down. A merchant selling over x402 can refuse agents that defaulted or give better prices for a clean history.

Where $PRIORS Sits in the Flywheel

The team’s design uses $PRIORS in three ways.

  1. Owners stake it to open bigger lines.
  2. Protocol revenue buys it and parks it behind proven agents.
  3. Protocol revenue also adds permanent liquidity to its pool.

Owner staking is the one that matters and it isn’t live yet. A SeatVaultV5 root went on chain on Oct 9 but isn’t backing any agents so far. Once it opens, an owner stakes $PRIORS to open a line that starts at $50 and grows to $500 as the agent repays week-long loans. Part of the stake burns on a default. The line is funded in USDG, so lenders never depend on the token’s price. The team says it has been through two audits, but no report is public.

The two revenue engines went live on Oct 6. The buyer is built to swap fees into $PRIORS and park it behind higher-tier agents with no way to sell. The liquidity engine adds USDG to the $PRIORS pool permanently. I couldn’t confirm a buy as of Oct 13. The reserve did fall from $218 to $206 after Oct 7 while fees kept coming in, which suggests the sweep has started moving money out.

The team has also posted a design for credit sized to an agent’s verified income. If it ships, lenders would no longer sit last. Owners and backers would take the first loss and lenders would fund and price the rest. For now it’s a post, not a contract.

Robinhood Stocks as Collateral

Since Priors lives on Robinhood Chain that makes on-chain stocks the most natural collateral it could offer.

An owner can deposit one of 35 Robinhood stock tokens and borrow against it. Index funds, T-bills and megacaps lend at 50% of value and volatile names at 25% to 40%. Lines cap at $250 and new borrowing pauses on sharp price moves, splits or paused tokens.

Robinhood customers already hold these stocks and their agents already trade them, now the same stocks can back an agent’s working capital without being sold.

Since Oct 5 the vault also takes PT-USDG, a dollar token bought at a discount that redeems 1:1 in March 2027.

I think it’s pretty cool that the Priors team added Pendle here. An owner can use it to back their agent’s line at 50%, up to $250 and still earn the fixed yield as it drifts to par.

That fixes the most annoying part of backing an agent.

Collateral normally sits idle, and backing a line costs you whatever that money could have earned elsewhere. With PT-USDG the same dollar does both jobs. It’s also a much calmer asset to lend against than a stock or $PRIORS. It’s priced in dollars and drifts toward par by a known date, so a line backed by it is far less likely to get frozen by a sharp move.

It’s a good sign for the bigger thesis too as a credit layer is only worth something if other protocols plug into it.

The Risks

  • The default record is thin. There have been two defaults, both $5 treasury lines from the same owner, and the treasury covered them with no loss to lenders. The record still hasn’t faced a big loan, a long hold or a real drawdown that tests a backer’s capital.
  • Stock vault controls. Stock vault upgrades now go through a public timelock, but the 2-of-3 Safe can still pause the vault or change accepted stocks instantly. Robinhood can also pause or burn the underlying stock tokens.
  • The token’s main job isn’t working yet. The sweep has run since Oct 6 with only small sweeps and no visible buys. SeatVaultV5 went live around Oct 9 in an owner-only first stage, but it hasn’t opened any books.
  • The collateral is volatile. A sharp drop in $PRIORS shrinks what owners can back, and the depth guard pauses new backing when the pool is thin. That protects lenders but stalls growth when the token is weakest.
  • The raw score can be farmed. It costs pennies to inflate, and 10 seat agents already rely on it. Score v2 ignores those loans, but only merchants who check v2 are protected.
  • Demand and regulation are open questions. If owners keep pre-funding their agents, the record never gets deep. Unsecured lending to software will also likely draw regulators before it reaches scale

$PRIORS has traded between roughly $6M and $8M fully diluted around Oct 13, depending on the source, on thin liquidity. It hit about $13M on Oct 4 on the engine news and gave it back. For now it trades on narrative, not fees.

What I’m Watching

  • Agents graduating. The loan that sat open for 8 days was repaid on Oct 9, and a handful of agents now have a week-long repayment on their record. I want to see one of them earn a bigger line off the back of it.
  • A default that actually costs someone. The first two were $5 treasury lines, and the treasury covered the $10 the way it’s supposed to. I’ll trust the design more once a private backer eats a loss on a $50+ line.
  • The sweep’s first real buy. There have been small sweeps since Oct 6, but nothing big enough to show up in the token yet.
  • Someone staking $PRIORS behind their own agent. SeatVaultV5 is live in an owner-only first stage, but no books are open yet.
  • A loan bigger than $11. A $50 line exists now, but nobody has drawn more than $5 on it.
  • Merchants checking the score before they serve an agent. That’s when the record starts to matter to anyone outside Priors.

Where This Goes

The next step I’d watch for is an agent launchpad (lol).

Robinhood Chain already runs on launchpads like Pons, and Mosh wraps launches in market-making agents. Those are token launchpads.

An agent launchpad on Priors wouldn’t need a token at all.

It would launch the agent itself, with its ID, first line and first backers in one step. People could back new agents the way they back new tokens today and earn a share of the fees on the loans each agent repays.

Something close to that is already happening.

Orbio is an agent launchpad on Robinhood Chain. Minimum Viable Agent launched there and then went to Priors for credit on its own. It borrowed $5 USDG and posted about it on Oct 9. It repaid on Oct 10 after holding the loan for more than a week, then borrowed again.

The loan is tiny and the agent could have covered it anyway, but more importantly this shows the plumbing works more than it shows demand.

Still, an agent from another launchpad chose to build a Priors record on its own, and that’s the first step the launchpad idea needs.

Launchpads also suggest a second score. Priors already locks out every agent from an owner whose agent defaults. Flip that and you get a creator score. Builders whose agents repay could get bigger first lines on their next launch, and builders whose agents default would pay more on everything they ship. That could end up worth more than any single agent’s credit history. It isn’t on Priors’ roadmap as far as I know, but it’s where I think the design leads.

Closing Thoughts

The basics are live and lenders haven’t lost a dollar. What matters from here is whether the score becomes reliable and how this score can be plugged in across the entire RH on chain agentic economy.

A credit score only works if it holds up once real money is at stake. If Priors gets that right, other projects don’t have to build their own. Launchpads, merchants and agent platforms can read the score from the registry and build on top of it. Orbio (via that agent I shared above) already plugs in.

**That’s also why I wouldn’t judge Priors on the usual buyback and burn math, but rather, on how much Priors tokens get staked and locked up behind real, reliable agents doing valid work.

**Priors lends small amounts for agent payments over x402 that cost cents. The pool has accrued about $186 in fees across 38,658 loans, and fees will stay small for a long time.

The bigger piece is Priors becoming the credit layer for agentic finance on Robinhood Chain.
