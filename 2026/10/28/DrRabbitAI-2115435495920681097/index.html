How to Make Nature- and Cell-Style Scientific Figures with AI (Free Templates, Growing to 1,000)

发表于2026-10-28|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @DrRabbitAI（Roxy） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

Run the same prompt 10 times and you get 10 different figures. Arrows point the wrong way, labels come and go, and none of them is quite usable.

There’s a deeper problem too. Most of us can tell our figures look like PowerPoint clip art next to the ones in Nature or Cell, but we can’t say exactly why. And if you can’t name the difference, you can’t fix it in a prompt.

Today I want to share an open-source repo that solves both: free prompt templates learned from open-access Nature and Cell figures, updated continuously on its way to 1,000.

What’s inside:

  1. How the repo learns from top journals
  2. How the templates work
  3. How to use them
  4. Four prompt breakdowns you can copy
  5. Why they work, and whether AI figures are allowed in papers

First, the prompts learn from top-journal figures. They are based on open-access schematics from Nature- and Cell-family journals, and they write those journals’ choices of color, layout, line weight and labeling directly into the prompt.

This is the part I like most. If you’re new to writing papers, borrowing the palette and style of top journals is the safest place to start.

Second, every prompt is a template. The templates cover 8 fields: biomedicine, materials & energy, earth & environment, AI, engineering, chemistry & instruments, plant science, and social science. Every prompt comes with a technique note and [ ] placeholders: find the figure type you need, replace the parts in [ ] with your own topic, and run it.

The library keeps growing, with new templates added regularly on the way to 1,000. Bookmark it ：awesome-scientific-figure-prompts

If you use Gemini or YILA., you can copy a prompt and go.

How to use the templates

Pick the template closest to the figure you need. Replace the text inside [ ] with your own subject, structures or steps, then delete the brackets. Keep everything else.

Keep the text inside the figure to short English labels. They come out far more reliably than sentences.

Each example below shows only the content part of the prompt. The full version in the repo adds a style paragraph that makes the results more consistent.

Four prompt breakdowns you can copy

Four figures from four different fields. Under each heading: one technique worth borrowing, the prompt, and the figure it produced.

Graphical abstract: Tumor-targeted nanoparticle drug delivery

Technique: Three stages from left to right for what happens in the body, plus one magnified inset for what happens inside the cell. Macro and micro in a single figure.

Signaling pathway: cGAS–STING innate immunity

Technique: Write the pathway as a numbered list, one relationship per line. Written this way, the figure came out right on the first try.

Cross-section: Lithium-ion battery charge and discharge

Technique: A cross-section with flow arrows: ions through the electrolyte and electrons through the external circuit, drawn as two separate paths.

Architecture diagram: Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

Technique: Architecture diagrams work like signaling pathways: list every step, and the modules don’t get wired wrong.

Why these prompts work

The style comes from real journal figures. Top-journal schematics look polished mostly because of restraint: a white background with light panels to group regions; three or four colors, each with one fixed meaning; thin, even lines with no 3D glow or shadows; short labels instead of sentences; and circular magnified insets that connect scales. All of this is written into the prompts.

Complex relationships become a numbered list. Most of the randomness comes from leaving too much for the model to guess. Written as one paragraph, a PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway came out looking like a journal figure but wired wrong: S6K inhibiting 4E-BP1 instead of mTORC1, and PTEN in the wrong place.

Rewritten as a numbered list, the same model drew the cGAS–STING and TGF-beta pathways correctly on the first try.

Less text. The more text you ask for inside a figure, the more garbled it gets. With explanations at every step, a single-cell RNA-seq workflow came out with several misspellings. With only five step names, it came out clean.

Can you use AI figures in papers?

Many people assume AI can never be used for scientific figures. It depends on the type of figure and where it goes.

Data figures: never. Micrographs, blots and plots of results must come from real data. Having an image model draw a data figure is fabrication. Plotting your real data with code is a different thing and is fine.

Schematics: it depends on where they’re used. Lab meetings, proposals, defenses, grant applications, teaching slides, posters and science communication are all fine, and that’s what these templates are mainly for.

Journal submissions: policies differ. Nature Portfolio journals currently don’t accept images made with generative AI, schematics included. Science journals require prior permission from the editors. Elsevier journals, including Cell, don’t allow general-purpose AI image tools for graphical abstracts. Always check your target journal’s author guidelines.

Finally

I’ve come to think many skills are learned the same way. To write a good paper, you don’t start from zero: you read enough papers in authoritative journals to see how they frame questions and build arguments. Scientific figures are no different. Study top-journal figures: imitate first, then surpass. This repo makes the imitation step easy.
