Who wins agentic commerce?

发表于2026-03-22|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @antoniogm（Antonio García Martínez） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

This post is getting some traction on the timeline, particularly in light of last week’s announcement of MPP from Tempo/Stripe. It raises some interesting points, but also assumes some dicey conclusions. In the interests of absolute truth-seeking, let’s take it apart.

Open protocols don’t always win.
@samrags_ cites the historical analogy of AOL losing the consumer Internet vs. the chaotic, open stack of HTTP and TCP/IP, but open hasn’t always won in general.

The one place open certainly did not win is in the principal business model for the internet: ads. Google built an open ads ecosystem using composable design and open protocols and standards, but Facebook (and every other social media platform after) quite famously opted for a walled garden….and did just fine. Arguably, Facebook won in the end as Google’s open Web ads are now relatively less valuable and remnant, while the Instagram feed is premium real estate.

(Coincidentally, I’ve got a cameo in this drama as I bet my entire job on the programmatic path for Facebook, shipping their first real-time open ad exchange FBX…which did not endear me to management.)

Going even further and closer to home, open social protocols clearly lost, as Farcaster’s demise demonstrates. Open feels better because it appeals to an engineering aesthetic of modularity and the cyberpunk ethos of openness, but ‘open source’ as a business only works in very narrow software domains. Most everywhere else, the profit-maximizing strategy is erecting walls and tollbooths and enthroning network effects as much as possible.

**Who’s running the agent?
**@samrags_ implicitly assumes your average consumer will run AI agents like coders do, curating or interacting with a set of skills files, watching over an agent like a junior colleague and so on. That’s optimistic to say the least. There’ll be a consumer app experience inevitably…so what’s that then?

If OpenAI remains that largest consumer touchpoint (even if only as an entry point, like Google now), then it’s not clear why anything should be ‘open’ just as Facebook wholly owning 3B+ users meant they could build whatever walled garden they wanted. Google had to run an open playbook since most of the Web was ‘out there’ only being indexed by Google, but interacted with directly by users post-click. If OpenAI brings the Internet to the user and spoonfeeds it to them in AI form, why would OpenAI have to be open at all? Only a thriving and fragmented world of consumer AI apps (even if still using OpenAI models under the hood) would argue for something like an open world of autonomous agents using open protocols…and we’re not in that world yet.

**This assumes fungible, commodity commerce
**The examples cited by @samrags_ are fungible, functional goods like pizza or a supply-chain input. But the big-ticket items in e-commerce are the conspicuous consumption items of lifestyle and fashion, ones where the shopping is much of the consumer experience.

Put another way: consumers often don’t want to save time…they want to waste time. The engineering mindset that assumes efficiency is the only goal is bad consumer intuition.

Consider how in the year of our Lord 2026, brands are still paying fortunes to keep busy storefronts in Soho and Fifth Avenue, showcasing goods even if they’re purchased online later, as it’s a key part of the consumption ritual (and brand-building: we are the sort of shoe you can buy on Prince Street).

All the reasoning in the piece assumes some headless agent flow with undifferentiated goods competing on price and convenience alone, when that’s a relatively small fraction of the whole picture. In reality, it’s not just agents all the way up, and at some point you will encounter a human staring at pixels and making an emotion-driven decision and that human will be swayed by the online version of a Soho shop (and if OpenAI can’t build one, someone else will).

In that world of non-fungible goods and services–luxury handbags, watches, vacation trips with complex components like lodging and dining–it won’t be hyper-efficient agents haggling with each other about the fastest, cheapest way to make a pizza appear: there’ll be a human staring at a consumer experience, and that user is captured by someone who very much doesn’t want to let them go and doesn’t care about abstract values like openness.

Payments recapitulate protocols
If OpenAI manages to remain the AI one-stop-shop and somehow competing AI-enabled consumer experiences don’t appear, then OpenAI will have a monopsony, a stranglehold on demand (or to use Ben Thompson’s language, they’re an aggregator).

In that world, the only ‘openness’ they care about is having every source of product supply standardize themselves into a product feed (and payment mechanic) that OpenAI can harness to totally own the user and purchase funnel. And everyone will have to fall into line.

(To dip back to the ads example, note that Google never opened up AdWords to its programmatic open ecosystem…that was always a walled garden, because it was the only search engine that mattered and could dictate terms.)

Is it a wonder then that the competing commerce standards, ACP and UCP, reflect those dueling strategies?

ACP assumes the AI agent will control the entire consumer experience, and the only role of the retailer is cataloging and surfacing their goods for sale, while UCP (backed by Shopify naturally) is more about standardizing the various interacting nodes and is much less opinionated about how the consumer purchase funnel works.

To a very initial read, that means that payments in the ACP world (backed by Stripe alongside OpenAI) will bias to one form of payment (i.e MPP) and not actually care about interoperability much, while payment in the UCP world could indeed remain open (i.e. x402).

Here, @samrags_ makes an interesting historical analogy we’ll extend a bit:
UCP + x402 is like HTTP plus TCP/IP, which is how the open Web happened. Meanwhile, ACP + MPP is more the Apple App store model…and who is Tim Cook in this analogy?

What standard wins on the payment front then will be determined by who wins the tug of war to control the consumer experience itself: Will the commercial Web in fact be wrapped in a single AI-enabled experience or not?
