Why Specie Matters

发表于2026-04-29|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @MattDMark（Matt） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

By @mannyornothing

There is a category of business that nearly every bank claims to support yet very few actually serve well in practice. These are companies that operate across borders as a default. An importer in Miami sourcing from Shenzhen, an exporter in Mexico City selling into Frankfurt, a services firm in Lagos working with clients across continents, or a SaaS company billing in multiple currencies all share the same underlying constraint. Their financial activity is global, but the infrastructure available to them is not.

What they rely on today reflects that mismatch. On one side are legacy banking rails that were built decades ago and move slowly, often taking days to settle while layering fees at each step. On the other side are fintech tools that improve specific parts of the workflow but leave businesses stitching together multiple systems just to function. The result is not a single broken product but a fragmented stack that requires constant coordination.

Specie is trying to collapse that stack into a single system. What makes it interesting is not just the breadth of features, but the way it rethinks the underlying economics of how global B2B banking should work.

The problem that hasn’t been fixed

Cross-border B2B payments exceed $150 trillion annually yet the experience for most businesses remains consistently inefficient. This is not a new issue, and that persistence is part of the signal.

Transfers moving through the SWIFT system typically take between two and five business days to settle. Along the way, they pass through three to five intermediary banks, each taking a fee that is rarely visible upfront. Foreign exchange pricing compounds the problem with markups often ranging from 1% to 4% above mid-market rates depending on the corridor and the relationship with the bank.

In practical terms, a business sending $100,000 from the US to a supplier in Vietnam can lose $3,000 to $4,000 in combined wire fees and FX spread without knowing the exact cost until after the transfer completes. Receiving payments creates a similar layer of friction. Companies invoicing across multiple countries either maintain accounts in different currencies, each with its own requirements and overhead or push FX costs onto their customers which weakens their competitive position.

Treasury management adds another layer of complexity. Many businesses in the $5 million to $50 million revenue range still track cash positions across accounts and currencies using spreadsheets that are updated manually. This is less a failure of discipline and more a reflection of the tools available.

Over time fintech companies have improved individual parts of this system. Wise made FX pricing more transparent. Mercury modernized US startup banking. Brex and Ramp focused on cards and spend. Each of these products works well in isolation, but for a business operating across borders, the experience is still fragmented.

What remains missing is a system that treats global financial activity as the default case rather than an edge case.

Bringing the system into one place

Specie’s core insight is that the problem is not just about better tools, but about reducing the need for multiple tools altogether. For a finance team every additional platform introduces another login, another reconciliation step, and another point of failure.

Instead of optimizing a single function, Specie covers the full workflow of how a global business moves and manages money:

  • Payments across 124+ countries, using rails such as ACH, SEPA, PIX, SPEI, and SWIFT, with same-day settlement where supported
  • Receiving through virtual accounts that allow businesses to accept payments as if they were local in each region
  • FX conversion priced as low as 0.1% above mid-market rates, with transparent pricing rather than embedded spreads
  • Treasury visibility across currencies in a single dashboard, replacing manual tracking across accounts
  • Additional layers including corporate cards, invoicing, approval workflows, and integrations with systems like QuickBooks and Xero

Individually, none of these features are entirely new. What changes is the experience of having them integrated into a single system rather than spread across separate tools.

The more distinctive layer sits on top of this foundation.

Yield as a default, not a separate decision

Most B2B banking platforms treat yield as something separate from day-to-day operations. Businesses are typically required to move funds into dedicated accounts, accept constraints like minimum balances or lockups, and actively manage where idle cash sits.

Specie removes that distinction by making yield a default property of holding cash. Balances earn over 3% APY automatically backed 1:1 by US treasury reserves managed by BlackRock, without requiring any additional action.

The numbers are straightforward. A business holding $500,000 in working capital earns roughly $15,000 annually at a 3% yield. At $2 million, that increases to around $60,000. In traditional banking setups that same capital often generates little to no return.

What matters more than the absolute numbers is how this changes behavior. In most systems, there is a constant tradeoff between liquidity and yield. Businesses either keep funds accessible and earn nothing or move them into yield-generating products and give up flexibility. Managing that tradeoff introduces additional processes including forecasting and periodic fund movement.

By allowing cash to remain liquid while earning yield, Specie removes that layer entirely. The result is not just incremental income, but a simpler operating model for treasury.

This also reflects a different alignment. Traditional banks rely on the spread between what they pay depositors and what they earn on those deposits. Offering higher yield directly compresses that margin. Specie, by sourcing yield from treasury-backed instruments and monetizing through payments and FX, operates under a different set of incentives.

The infrastructure shift underneath

While the product presents itself as a standard banking interface, the underlying system appears to rely on stablecoin-based infrastructure.

References to private payment networks, regulated ramping partners, and treasury-backed yield linked to BlackRock suggest integration with tokenized treasury products such as BUIDL or similar instruments. This architecture is becoming more common among newer financial platforms.

The shift matters because it changes the economics. Traditional correspondent banking involves multiple intermediaries and delayed settlement. Stablecoin-based systems allow for direct transfers, faster settlement, and significantly lower cost structures.

This shows up clearly in the product. FX at around 0.1% compares to the typical 1% to 4% range in legacy systems. Settlement times move from multiple business days to same-day or near-instant in supported corridors. Yield becomes viable because the system does not carry the same overhead.

From the user’s perspective, none of this requires interacting with crypto directly. The infrastructure changes, but the experience remains familiar. The benefit is expressed in cost, speed, and efficiency rather than technology.

The real challenge: 124 countries

Operating across more than 124 countries is not just a feature. It is the core execution challenge.

Each country introduces its own regulatory requirements, payment systems, and compliance obligations. A transfer from the US to Nigeria operates under different conditions than one from Germany to Brazil, both technically and legally.

Supporting this requires a network of:

  • on and off-ramp partners across jurisdictions
  • local payment rail integrations
  • FX liquidity across multiple currency pairs
  • compliance systems tailored to each market

This is not a problem that scales purely through software. It resembles a logistics network, where each additional market requires incremental work and creates incremental value. Over time, the density and reliability of these connections become the real moat.

Positioning and competition

The B2B fintech space is crowded, with players like Mercury, Brex, Ramp, Wise, and Payoneer each owning part of the workflow.

Specie’s position comes from combining these layers into a single system designed specifically for globally operating businesses. The bet is that integration across payments, banking, treasury, and yield is more valuable than best-in-class performance in any single category.

That is a coherent thesis, but it shifts the challenge toward execution. Competing across multiple domains requires depth in each, and the question is whether a newer platform can match incumbents that have focused narrowly and built over longer periods.

What to watch

As the product develops, a few practical questions will determine whether the model holds:

  • how consistent settlement speed and FX pricing are across different corridors
  • how the treasury-backed yield behaves in practice, particularly under varying liquidity conditions
  • how effectively compliance is managed across a large number of jurisdictions without adding friction

These are operational questions, but they define whether the system works in reality.

The broader shift

Specie reflects a broader transition toward financial products built on stablecoin infrastructure while maintaining familiar interfaces. The underlying rails change, but the user experience remains aligned with traditional expectations.

Regulatory frameworks such as the GENIUS Act in the US and MiCA in Europe are beginning to formalize this space. At the same time, stablecoin settlement volumes have already exceeded the combined volumes of Visa and Mastercard, and industry leaders have suggested that a significant share of new neobanks will be built on these rails.

What has been missing is the application layer that translates these advantages into usable products for businesses that care about outcomes rather than underlying technology.

Specie is one of the more complete attempts to build that layer for global B2B finance. The question is not whether the underlying economics are better, but whether the system can be executed reliably across 124 countries. If it can, the advantages in cost, speed, and capital efficiency are difficult for traditional models to replicate.

That is what makes it worth watching closely.
