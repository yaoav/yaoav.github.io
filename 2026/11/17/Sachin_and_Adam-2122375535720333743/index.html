Yes, That's a Hearing Aid

发表于2026-11-17|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @Sachin_and_Adam（Sachin and Adam） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

I am 29 and largely deaf in my left ear, though I wasn’t born this way. Two and a half years ago, I went to bed with completely normal hearing and woke up the next morning (the day of a friend’s wedding!) barely able to hear out of that ear. Otherwise, I felt completely fine. My hearing hasn’t improved since.

I’m writing about this for a few reasons: 1) if you’ve noticed my hearing aid and were too polite to ask me about it, here you go 2) I want to talk about a company that’s made my life better and that we subsequently invested in and 3) despite all this, I feel grateful and optimistic, and wanted to say why.

Trying to get my hearing back

When it happened, I assumed it was just earwax buildup. An urgent care visit confirmed that it was probably either that or something similarly harmless and temporary called eustachian tube dysfunction. When a week passed with no improvement, my mom texted her ENT friend about it. He responded that I needed to go to the hospital immediately if I wanted a chance at salvaging my hearing.

The hospital diagnosed me with sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSHL). It’s a relatively rare condition that involves sudden loss of hearing originating in the inner ear, often without an identifiable cause. One cause the doctors did want to rule out was a vestibular schwannoma, a tumor on the nerve involved in hearing and balance. Fortunately, I didn’t have one.

I went through several months of treatment involving oral steroids, intratympanic steroid injections (very fun shots injected directly through your eardrum), and 40 hours in a hyperbaric oxygen chamber, where I passed the time with lots of movies. This was all largely to no avail, although some of the movies were really good.

I don’t think the one week delay before I started treatment mattered, but who can tell – I don’t lose sleep over it. If you are ever suddenly unable to hear, though, don’t wait like I did. The steroid treatment works better the sooner you start.

Hearing aids I didn’t like

It unfortunately looked like my hearing loss would be permanent. For about a year, my response was mostly to refuse to wear a hearing aid despite being a solid candidate for one. My reasoning was that I still could hear well enough in most situations, given my right ear works perfectly well, and I didn’t want to look like I was 72.

There was also a practical problem with hearing aids. I mostly had trouble hearing in louder environments like restaurants, and these are the situations in which hearing aids have traditionally struggled most. They amplify all noise: the person talking to you, the fork clattering behind you, the other tables’ conversation. It’s very distracting and renders an aid nearly useless in these environments. I tried two different aids, several months apart, and returned both quickly.

One of the stranger ironies of hearing loss is that it frequently causes tinnitus. The leading theory is that when the brain suddenly lacks auditory input, it compensates by generating noise of its own. Mine is mostly a ringing, with the occasional pure tone, like a bell. Hearing aids provide more consistent auditory stimulation and can alleviate it. As my tinnitus got worse with my continued lack of stimulus, I became more tempted to try a hearing aid again.

Eventually, I pulled the trigger when I learned of the Phonak Spheres, hearing aids that use AI to separate speech from background noise. This is a perfect problem for AI: train a neural network on noisy recordings paired with clean speech and it learns to preserve voices while filtering out the surrounding racket.

I bought one and somewhat begrudgingly used it for about a year. It helped with the tinnitus and was better in loud environments than my prior aids but still not great. The sound felt artificial, like all audio was being recorded and then played back to me.

I also didn’t love meeting people with my aid in. Sometimes I could see the moment they noticed it mid-conversation and started half listening as they wondered what was going on with my ear. I’d prefer they pay attention to whatever incredibly interesting thing I’m presumably saying.

Occasionally, there were also funnier interactions. Several people asked whether it was “that new AI wearable.” I’m still not sure what product they were referencing. One woman asked me to take it out because she didn’t want me recording our conversation (don’t worry, it can’t record). Mostly, though, I didn’t like dealing with all this for a product that still wasn’t particularly helpful in the louder situations where I needed it most.

A hearing aid I actually like

Then I came across Fortell, an early-stage startup in New York that was getting a lot of buzz for making a far better AI hearing aid than my Sphere. The team also caught my attention. Their chief scientist, Igor, is a Harvard physics PhD + a Juilliard-trained concert pianist. Likely a pretty good background for someone trying to make a hearing aid sound natural. I made an appointment at their NYC clinic, got one properly tuned to my hearing loss profile, and then took it home to demo in the best way I knew how: a visit to a favorite loud restaurant and then a favorite loud bar with my wife.

We went to Great NY Noodletown (in case you like Cantonese) and then to Dead Rabbit (in case you like Guinness). These are exactly the kinds of places where my earlier aids had struggled. Fortell lets you toggle between modes, and the AI mode was incredible at separating her voice from the noise around us. There’s also a front-facing AI mode that only amplifies voices directly in front, which was even better. Everything sounded clear and much more natural than it had with my Sphere. For the first time since losing my hearing, I was getting something close to how I remembered things before my loss.

Much of this comes down to Fortell’s tech. They designed their own custom ASIC (manufactured by TSMC) to run their neural networks continuously on-device, performing real-time audio source separation with very low latency. But what’s particularly impressive is what those models can distinguish. They don’t just separate speech from background noise (which my Sphere could also do). They use spatial information to tell competing voices apart, allowing me to hear my wife rather than the guy yapping two tables over.

Since I’ve had it, the sound quality and speech separation have improved further through over-the-air software updates. I’ve also found the team to be unusually responsive to my questions and requests, one of the advantages of selling directly through their own clinics rather than relying on third-party audiology practices.

Naturally, all this made me want to invest, and luckily we found a way to put in a small check. That makes me biased, but these hearing aids really do seem to be blowing away the broader audiology world and Fortell has published their own research where their aids far outperform a competing AI hearing aid (reported to be the Sphere I had previously been wearing). One of the NYU researchers told Wired he had never seen results that clear-cut in his career.

Reasons to be optimistic

It’s hard not to feel good about the future when I use a product every day that didn’t exist until recently, and keeps getting better every couple months.

Beyond improving its own hearing aids, I suspect Fortell is helping get the slow-moving incumbents off their asses. The “Big Five” hearing aid manufacturers have long been associated with high prices and minimal innovation. Yet in the past few months, all five have launched new AI hearing aids. Perhaps they’re simply capitalizing on the same advances in chips and AI that made Fortell’s technology possible. But I suspect the added competition from a dynamic startup has given them some needed pressure. While I’m selfishly rooting for Fortell to win, as a consumer, I’m most excited about the better hearing aids this competition will produce regardless of manufacturer.

There’s progress beyond hearing aids, too. Researchers are working on ways to regenerate the inner ear’s sensory hair cells, the cells that may have been damaged or lost in my own hearing loss. This includes experiments that reprogram other cells into hair-cell-like cells in adult mice. Further along, in April, the FDA granted accelerated approval to Otarmeni, a gene therapy for a rare inherited form of deafness. It doesn’t treat my kind of hearing loss, but the fact that we can treat any form of deafness in this way is incredible.

I don’t know whether someone will figure out how to restore my hearing. But I am highly confident that the options available to me in 10 years will be substantially better than the ones I have today.

The loss still sucks, and the tinnitus can be maddening. But it’s also easy to look at my situation and see how much worse it could have been. I didn’t have a tumor and I don’t have the vertigo that often accompanies sudden hearing loss.

There are even a few benefits. I get a good excuse to choose my seat at dinner parties. I’m a side sleeper, so when I put my good ear against the pillow, the outside world is completely blocked out without needing a sound machine. I even get to selectively ignore things I don’t want to hear and blame it on my hearing loss.

For now, I have one good ear and another that works well with an aid I actually want to wear. I’m very grateful for that.
