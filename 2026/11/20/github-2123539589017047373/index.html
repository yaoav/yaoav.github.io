Full Security Guide: Grok Bot (8 Settings to Change Today)

发表于2026-11-20|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @github（GitHub） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

Grok Bot is xAI’s system of AI teammates. Each Bot can sign into your apps, use your browser and pay with your saved card.

What most guides skip: all of your Bots share one computer. This guide covers the 8 settings that keep that from becoming a problem.

Elon wants you to build a whole company out of Grok Bots.

Before you hire 12 of them, read one line from xAI’s own documentation:

“Do not use separate Bots as a security boundary.”

Your “research bot,” your “finance bot” and your “inbox bot” are not 3 employees in 3 offices. They’re 3 employees sharing one desk, one browser and one set of keys.

Grok Bot guides with millions of views will show you how to set it up. This one shows you how to set it up without handing every bot the keys to everything. Everything below comes from xAI’s official docs (last updated Nov 13, 2026). Save it before your next “Always allow.”

The Short Version

  • All your Bots run on ONE shared cloud computer. Files, browser sessions and logins are shared across all of them.
  • Deleting a Bot does NOT delete its files or sign it out of anything.
  • A card saved in Vault pays at checkout with no approval each time. And in Grok Bot app chats, the Bot can see the card number and security code.
  • “Always allow” quietly turns one approval into a permanent rule.
  • The fix is 8 settings and habits. Steps below.

1. The One-Computer Problem

Each Bot gets its own name, its own chat, its own personality. It feels like separate workers.

It isn’t. Per xAI’s docs, all of your Bots share one cloud computer assigned to your account. Files, browser sessions and command line credentials on that computer are available across your entire Bot roster.

What that means in practice:

  • You sign your “travel bot” into your airline account. Your “inbox bot” is now on a computer that’s signed into your airline account.
  • Your “finance bot” downloads a bank statement to the shared workspace. Every other Bot can reach that file.
  • A Bot you set up for a quick test can use every login your other Bots created.

If you want truly separate computers and credentials, the docs’ answer is a separate Cursor user account, not a separate Bot.

2. Deleting a Bot Doesn’t Clean Up After It

This one surprises people. From the docs:

“Deleting a Bot does not remove shared-computer files or browser sessions.”

So the Bot is gone, but

  • the websites it signed into are still signed in
  • the files it downloaded are still in the workspace
  • the apps it connected may still be authorized in the source service

Cleanup is manual. Section 6 has the checklist.

3. Your Card, Your Bot, No Questions Asked

Grok Bot has a Vault for logins and cards. The good part: logins from Vault and secrets entered in Secure Forms are filled in without being shown to the model.

The part to know before you save a card:

  • A card saved in Vault fills in at checkout without a separate approval each time.
  • In chats in the Grok Bot app, a Bot can see the number and security code of a card you saved in Vault, so it can type them at checkout.

Now combine that with Section 1: one shared computer, many Bots.

If you want to approve every payment, the docs are clear: don’t save the card. Leave “Securely save to Bot’s vault” unchecked when you enter it, or remove it in Settings → Vault.

4. “Always Allow” Is a Rule, Not a Click

When a Bot wants to do something sensitive, you get an approval card:

  • Allow once: does it now, saves nothing.
  • Always allow: does it now AND saves an Allow rule, so similar actions run on their own next time.
  • Deny: doesn’t do it.

Worth knowing:

  • Each “Always allow” saves a rule. At 20 Allow rules, the next one replaces your oldest.
  • An approval doesn’t reverse work already completed.
  • “Ask first” beats “Allow automatically” when both match.
  • Auto Review only counts what YOU typed as your request. Text from an email or web page doesn’t, which is exactly what you want against prompt injection.

5. Your Own Computer Is a Separate Switch

The shared Bot computer runs in the cloud. Access to the laptop in front of you is a separate setting, and it’s easy to give away by accident.

The first time a Bot asks to run a command on your computer, you’ll see a prompt to allow Grok Bot and ALL Bots to run commands on your local computer. “Always allow” there applies to every Bot.

The docs’ own recommendation: use “Never allow” unless a Bot has a specific reason to work on your local files.

6. The Lockdown (Step by Step)

  1. Lock your laptop. Settings → Computer → Your computer → Execution on this computer → Never allow. Switch to “Ask every time” only for a task that truly needs local files.
  2. Keep Auto Review on. Settings → General → under Bot → Auto-review switch: ON. With it off, the Bot runs actions without checking them first.
  3. Write 3 “Ask first” rules. Custom Rules (under the Auto-review switch) → Add Rule. Keep each one narrow:
    “Ask first before sending any email, message or invitation.”
    “Ask first before any purchase, payment or transfer.”
    “Ask first before deleting or overwriting files.”
    Avoid broad rules like “allow everything in the browser.” The docs say so too.
  4. Audit your “Always allow” history. Open Custom Rules and delete every Allow rule you don’t remember creating.
  5. Decide on your card. Want an approval for every payment? Settings → Vault → remove the saved card. Next time, leave “Securely save to Bot’s vault” unchecked.
  6. Never paste secrets into chat. Passwords, one-time codes and API keys go only into the Secure Form or secure secret card when the Bot asks. Those are masked and excluded from the transcript. Ordinary chat is not.
  7. Use “Take over” for the scary steps. For bank verification, 2FA prompts or CAPTCHAs, choose Take over, complete the step yourself, press I’m done, then tell the Bot to continue.
  8. Clean up after every project. Pause or delete routines you don’t need. Sign out of websites on the shared computer. Uninstall connectors AND revoke them in the source service (Google, Slack, etc.). Delete sensitive files from /workspace. Then hide or delete the Bot.

7. Work vs Personal: The Only Split That Works

Separate Bots are not separation. Per the docs, for a truly separate computer and credentials you need a separate Cursor user.

  • Work account: work logins, work connectors, work Bots.
  • Personal account: personal email, personal cards, personal Bots.
  • Never sign a work Bot into personal banking “just this once.” It’s one computer.

Sharing settings matter too:

  • A public share link copies a Bot’s configuration. It does not share your computer or logins, but don’t put secrets, customer data or internal URLs in a Bot you share.
  • A Team Bot is different: the apps, secrets, skills and files its owner adds work in every teammate’s chat. Only add what you’re happy for everyone on the team to use.

Running Grok Bot for a team? Two more lines from the docs:

  • Network Controls are Enterprise only. Teams without a network policy default to allow-all: Bot computers can reach any destination.
  • The team model allowlist does not govern Grok Bot. It uses xAI’s own models and may use third-party models regardless of your model access settings.

8. The Least-Privilege Checklist (Screenshot This)

  • Connect only the tools a workflow needs.
  • Start with read-only tasks and drafts.
  • Keep sending, publishing, purchasing, deleting and production changes behind approval.
  • Use scoped service accounts where the app supports them.
  • Review connectors and routines every week.
  • Pause a routine when the workflow changes.
  • Keep source links and an action log for important decisions.

All 7 come straight from xAI’s own “least-privilege setup” list. Most people skip it.

The Bottom Line

Grok Bot is genuinely useful. A “company made of bots” is a fun idea.

Just remember what that company really is: a dozen employees, one desk, one browser, one key ring, and a corporate card in the drawer.

LOCK THE DRAWER FIRST.
