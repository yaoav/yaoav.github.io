190M Views of Complete Insanity. Here’s My Picsart AI Video Playbook

发表于2026-11-20|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @JamalXBT（Jamal） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

A helicopter crew pours corn into a volcano. The volcano sends back popcorn. A fighter jet leaves a cinema screen and flies over the audience. A tower of people bends toward a balcony because somebody wants tea.

Six AI clips in my research archive had accumulated 190,260,749 Instagram views when their counters were saved. The interesting part is how little explanation the scenes need. You understand the setup, anticipate a consequence, and stay to see how ridiculous it gets.

I’d build a small video studio around that kind of storytelling: an AI assistant to develop the scenes, Picsart to generate the visuals, and a clear offer for whoever needs the finished videos.

The $10K is a monthly revenue target in the business plan below. The view counts belong to the original Instagram posts, recorded on November 9 and 15, 2026. What follows is my proposed workflow for making original work with Picsart.

1. Start with the cup of tea

The biggest clip in this selection has the smallest objective.

In @cain.aii’s Reel, two men at the top of an enormous human tower lean toward a balcony for chai. The tower bends, they reach the cup, and the structure returns upright. The saved counter: 52,330,531 views and 984,842 likes.

@cain.aii · 52,330,531 Instagram views · counter saved 2026-11-09 ·

AI created the impossible bending crowd. The everyday objective makes that impossible image readable: they want a drink. You don’t need to understand the setting or hear a line of dialogue.

The useful creative question is: what ordinary desire could require a completely unreasonable solution?

Make coffee. Deliver lunch. Find a parking space. Collect a parcel. Choose one, give it a visible obstacle, and design the ending before asking a model to animate anything.

2. Give the viewer something specific to wait for

@realisticaivid’s highway scene had 49,940,513 views and 535,529 likes. A container starts tipping beside a bright green Lamborghini. The doors open, the occupants move, and the car escapes the falling container.

@realisticaivid · 49,940,513 Instagram views · counter saved 2026-11-15 ·

AI generated the vehicle movements and the near miss. The container’s angle makes the question immediate: will it hit the car? The clip keeps that relationship visible long enough for the escape to matter.

That gives you a useful brief for your own scene: one threat, one subject, one visible escape. A complicated backstory would only delay the part people came to see.

Keep the camera where the consequence can be understood. If the crucial object disappears during the action, the viewer has to guess what happened.

3. Make the ending change the scene

The two volcano clips use the same visual world and different rewards.

In one @scalefiction Reel, corn goes into the crater and popcorn comes back into the helicopter. The saved counter was 38,975,882 views and 1,889,114 likes. AI animated the transformation and filled the cabin with its consequences.

@scalefiction · 38,975,882 Instagram views · counter saved 2026-11-09 ·

Another Reel turns a bag of raw chicken into fried drumsticks, then brings them back to the crew for a taste. That one had 30,276,274 views and 511,207 likes. Its caption explicitly credits Seedance 2.5 on Dreamina.

@scalefiction · 30,276,274 Instagram views · counter saved 2026-11-15 ·

The scenes finish the experiment. You see what came back and how the people react. That gives the viewer a reason to stay beyond the first spectacular frame.

Reference frames: setup, transformation, payoff. @scalefiction; source footage, not a new Picsart generation.

For a series, keep a recognizable location and recurring people, then change the experiment and the consequence. A floating laundromat that washes clouds could generate several stories. Every episode still needs its own ending.

4. Change what the audience thinks they’re watching

@neuralbackstage’s crocodile scene had 13,499,942 views and 133,317 likes. A woman in a yellow jacket films an approaching crocodile. The encounter escalates, then the camera reveals a studio and crew around the scene. AI generated both the apparent danger and the fictional behind-the-scenes reveal.

@neuralbackstage · 13,499,942 Instagram views · counter saved 2026-11-15 ·

The fighter-jet cinema clip from @realisticaivid had 5,237,607 views and 45,132 likes. The AI-generated aircraft crosses the boundary between the projected film and the auditorium. The audience and camera react as the jet passes overhead.

@realisticaivid · 5,237,607 Instagram views · counter saved 2026-11-15 ·

A reveal gives the opening a second meaning. A boundary crossing turns a familiar room into the action itself. Both are useful structures for an original brief: establish what feels normal, then change one rule in a way the viewer can follow.

Reference frames: the aircraft crosses from the projected film into the auditorium. @realisticaivid.

These are observations from a selected group of successful clips. They tell me what to test; they don’t establish why the algorithm distributed them or predict what my next upload will earn.

5. Turn the references into your own brief

Here’s the research prompt I’d give an assistant with access to the reference videos:

I want the assistant to analyse the sequence and propose new scenes. Replacing a crocodile with a shark while copying the rest of a creator’s film isn’t much of a creative brief.

My first test would be a night cleaner closing an empty cinema. A tiny cloud escapes a bucket and rains into a single seat. The cleaner calmly opens an umbrella over it and goes back to work.

It has an ordinary job, an impossible interruption and a reaction that completes the joke. It also gives the model a much smaller coordination problem than a crowded street full of moving people.

You can work directly in Picsart’s AI Video Generator. Choose text-to-video for an idea, or image-to-video when you already have a useful opening frame. Reference inputs and output settings depend on the selected model.

I’d start with one short scene. A ten-episode season can wait until the first ending works.

7. Ask AI to plan the shot before buying motion

For the cinema cleaner, I’d send:

Approve the composition first. Can you see the cleaner, bucket, cloud and seat without searching? Does the final action make sense from the same angle?

Then generate the motion. If the model can’t manage the full sequence, divide it into two shots: the cloud appearing, then the cleaner’s reaction. Keep the same approved character and room references.

An assistant can prepare prompts and call the available generation tools. You still need to watch the output: did the cloud emerge from the bucket, did the umbrella stay above the seat, and did the ending survive the edit?

For a longer series, record the character’s appearance, recurring locations, camera style and approved references. That reduces unnecessary reinvention, while visual checks catch the changes that references fail to prevent.

8. Count the failed takes

A cheap render becomes an expensive habit when you keep buying another one without changing the scene.

Use this production instruction:

Picsart’s MCP documentation provides free catalog and cost-check tools, including picsart_model_params and picsart_preflight. Generation spends credits. That makes checking a request before running it part of the workflow.

For planning, I’d count generation, rejected attempts, sound, editing and my own production time. If I budget $15 per accepted video, twelve videos consume $180 before the rest of the business costs. The $15 is a planning allowance to test against actual usage, not a Picsart tariff.

The number I’d want after the pilot is simple: what did it take to deliver one video I’d put my name on?

9. Build an offer beside the videos

An entertaining clip can demonstrate your creative ability. A client needs to know what they can buy.

My starting offer would be a monthly pack for one kind of business: twelve original short videos, three recurring formats, two opening options for each concept, and one agreed revision round. Product facts and brand references come from the client. Paid-ad usage and extra revisions get agreed separately.

Here’s an illustrative $10K monthly revenue target:

→ 5 monthly clients × $1,600 = $8,000

→ 4 additional campaign videos × $500 = $2,000

→ Total: $10,000 in revenue before costs and tax

That means delivering 64 accepted videos a month if every monthly pack contains twelve. At the hypothetical $15 production allowance, media production alone would use $960. Sales, subscriptions, revisions, admin and your time still have to fit. The workload is worth calculating before selling the fifth pack.

I’d start with a paid pilot for one client, measure the delivery time, and use the result to price the next offer. A restaurant might want an absurd food experiment. A cinema might want a fictional screen-escape scene. The creative idea needs to lead toward something that business actually sells.

There’s also Earn with Picsart. Its official page describes campaigns for original content made with eligible Picsart tools, posted on your own social accounts, with rewards tied to campaign rules and engagement. Check the specific brief before producing for it. Original Instagram reference views can’t be substituted for campaign performance.

10. Run a small production test

For the first week, I’d choose one audience and develop six original concepts. Write the opening and payoff for each, then make the simplest three. Record the full spend and the time spent reviewing them.

Next, I’d publish the accepted work with appropriate AI disclosure and compare the audience response. Look at viewing time and shares when the platform supplies them, alongside profile visits, clicks and enquiries. Keep publication age in mind when comparing posts.

Then make a follow-up to the strongest idea, changing one major element. At the same time, put the finished examples into a clear pilot offer for relevant businesses. Audience testing and customer conversations answer different questions; I’d want evidence from both.

The six references gave me 190 million reasons to study the storytelling. They didn’t hand me a business. The business starts when I can repeatedly make an original scene worth watching and deliver it to someone who has a reason to pay.

Try building your first original scene with Picsart: https://picsart.go.link/5ZyeT

Write the ending. Make one good video. Sell the next one.
