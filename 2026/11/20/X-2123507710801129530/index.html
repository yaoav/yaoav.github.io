Replit's agent swarm built a monitoring tool in 2 days for $3,000. I'd write the finish line first

发表于2026-11-20|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @X（X） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

TL;DR: Replit launched Missions this week. You give it an outcome, a spending cap and a list of things it has to ask you about, then a swarm of agents works toward it. Replit says a mission built its own monitoring platform, DataCat, in 2 days inside a $3,000 budget. The part I’d copy isn’t the swarm. It’s the agreement you write before anything starts, because an AI judge grades the work against it and the work stops when the money runs out, finished or not.

What Replit launched

Missions went live on November 17. Replit’s DataCat post opened with “The leading observability platform wants $83,700/yr just to monitor your app” and passed 50K views within hours. Amjad Masad’s post said Missions “can spin up a swarm of agents to replicate any type of software of any complexity.” That one picked up about 38K views and 180 bookmarks in a few hours.

The setup is different from a normal agent chat. Before work starts you agree on a goal, success criteria, a spending limit and which actions need your permission. Then agents work in parallel and an independent AI judge checks results against your criteria.

A mission keeps going until one of three things happens. The criteria are met. It needs your input. Or the budget runs out, and in Replit’s words, “work stops with the outcome still unresolved.”

The DataCat numbers, and what they don’t say

Replit wanted to bring application monitoring in house. The mission built dashboards, alerts and tracing in 2 days with a $3,000 budget. When they planted a database bug, DataCat raised an alert in 38 seconds against a 60 second target. Over a 3 hour load test it stored 26 million activity records and 4.4 million log entries with no data loss, and passed all 51 correctness and alert checks.

They also compared it to a build from the same starting prompt using /goal in Claude Code. DataCat took in traces about 5x faster (49,000 a second vs 9,800) and passed 51 of 56 API compatibility checks vs 45. The Claude Code build covered more product areas and took in logs faster.

Fair caveats, some from Replit itself: one run per setup, Replit testing Replit’s product, and no comparison against the hosted service they wanted to replace. So I read it as “this can work”, not “this always works”.

Why the agreement is the real product

A swarm will happily spend money on the wrong goal. More agents in parallel just get you to the wrong place faster.

If the goal is “win our first 10 paying enterprise customers”, setup asks what counts. Does a paid pilot count? Or only signed annual contracts above some minimum? That one answer changes which accounts the agents chase.

And because the judge grades against your criteria, a line like “make it fast” gives it nothing to grade. “Alert within 60 seconds of a database failure” does.

The approvals matter just as much. Replit’s example caps a mission at $1,000 and makes the agent ask before it contacts prospects, shares a proposal, changes customer facing sales material or makes product commitments. The product page also lists “Approve before publishing” and “Approve any budget increase”.

How I’d write the finish line

If we’re looking at handing something like this a real project at Channel Automation, say a reporting screen our home improvement customers keep asking for, I’d write the agreement before I pick a number:

  1. A one sentence goal a customer would recognise. Not “build reporting”. More like “an owner can see this month’s jobs by status in one screen”.

  2. Three to five pass or fail checks with numbers. Load time, error rate, which data has to show up.

  3. What it can’t touch. Live customer data, billing, anything that messages a homeowner.

  4. What needs my OK. Publishing, any budget increase, anything a customer sees.

  5. A budget I’m fine losing. If it stops at 80% done, is the 80% still useful to us? If not, the goal is too big.

  6. Who signs off. The judge is an AI. Someone on our team still reads the result before it ships.

What this means for you

If you’re a solo builder: try it on something you’d never get to otherwise, like an internal tool. Keep the first cap small. Replit says messages you send during an active mission count toward its budget, so don’t chat with it all day.

If you lead a team: the hard part moves from writing code to writing acceptance criteria. Whoever writes the clearest pass or fail checks just got more valuable.

If you sell software: “replicate any type of software” is a bold claim, but it’s worth taking seriously. If a swarm can copy your product from the outside in two days, your moat is the customers, the data and the workflows people already trust.

If you’re learning: practice writing success criteria in plain words. Every one of these tools leans on that.

The line I’d save: a swarm doesn’t fix a vague goal, it just spends faster. Write the finish line before you write the budget.
