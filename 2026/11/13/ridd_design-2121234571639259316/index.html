Why designers will not be replaced by AI

发表于2026-11-13|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @ridd_design（Ridd 🤿） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

Open design roles just hit a record low, and some are concluding that designers are toast. After a year of running a scout network for hiring designers, I couldn’t disagree more. Here’s why:

Before you roll your eyes and think “this guy is simply talking his book”, entertain me for a sec.

Theo Browne is the founder of T3 Code, an open-source app for working with agents. He was recently told the app could do with some UI work. With no full-time designer on the team, he set up a fascinating experiment:

  • An AI agent took screenshots of the app, and a panel of models scored its UI out of 10 against Claude Code and Codex.
  • He’d then get the models to iterate on that feedback.
  • He repeated this process until all models gave a 10 out of 10 (it took 18 hours and 30 rounds).

Here are the results:

His conclusion makes sense, but I’d add to it. A skilled product designer could (and should) go much further. I personally use Conductor and have always felt that this particular structure (side panels, chat UI, etc.) could do with some work. Visual changes don’t cut it for me.

You might tell me that it’s only a matter of time before the models get there. Here’s why I disagree:

  1. Excellence requires instinct: A lot of design work is hard to measure and relies on intuition. The inputs and outputs are difficult for an LLM to structure and verify (e.g. a visual change might look better, but it doesn’t necessarily make a product work better).
  2. Understanding requires pushback: A skilled designer needs empathy not just to do what a user says, as an AI would, but to go a level deeper and understand the problem in detail. In Theo’s experiment a skilled designer might push back and ask: Is the current layout the right one?
  3. Mastery is in the details: The final 10% of a task is where mastery begins, and where it gets subjective. What you deem excellent someone else might deem average. There isn’t a single right answer aside from knowing your users well enough to predict their preferences.

The bar for what we consider “excellent design” has been raised, as I’ll show in the next section.

Five excellent design portfolios

1. Logan Brannen

A lot of portfolios feel like you’ve seen versions of them before. That leads hiring managers to think they were one-shotted by AI.

Logan’s catches you off guard. The first thing you see is Logan in an outfit you can change. You’re instantly hooked and want to discover more.

You open his first project and you’re in a stunning hero section. The work itself is very original. Strong visual, motion and branding skills are on display.

He doesn’t need a fancy title: the work shows skills that don’t fit neatly in a box.

2. Preston Booth

Preston’s portfolio immerses you in his world, quite literally. The first thing you see is a 3D render of his real-life desk and shelf.

His digital self follows you around as you explore his bookshelf. His about page has a scrunched up paper mache cursor over his name. All of this says to me: “I’m a designer that’s in the details”.

He also includes a short video on the story of his career:

My first day of class we watched the iconic IDEO shopping cart video. Watching that video made me realise I didn’t want to just build what someone else decided; I wanted to be the creative visionary making those decisions and designing things from the ground up.

In a few short seconds he’s told you more about himself than any resume could.

3. Daniella Marynova

Daniella’s portfolio leads with the work and does not disappoint. The vibe is playful: you discover new details as you dig further.

Hover over her name and all the little characters animate. The footer feels like a game of fidget spinners. Switch the lights off and on in the bottom right-hand corner.

I also love how she describes herself:

Simplicity is my superpower. I turn complex ideas into experiences users love and investors trust. I help SaaS founders craft story-driven brands and products

No buzzwords or AI speak. She’s staying true to her superpower: keeping it simple.

4. Bogdan Stefanovic

Bogdan doesn’t have big-name companies on his resume, so he leads with his personal projects. He’s very deliberate about the ordering in the left-hand column.

The visual details are immaculate.

You can tell he spent a long time on the buttons and their hover states. Open a project and the page fades back whilst the modal slides in. Scroll past the end of a case study and the next one opens. He’s made browsing delightful and easy.

5. Sean Smith

Sean has created a unique world and aesthetic for himself.

His latest experience was at Microsoft, where he was a principal designer. But he doesn’t lead with that. He calls himself a designer/builder and leads with Ghostties, his parallel agent terminal that he builds as a side project.

His moving headshot creates intrigue and makes you want to explore further.

Closing thoughts

Today’s tools unlock workflows we’d have deemed impossible until recently. Every portfolio above shows what a skilled designer can do with AI.

But what doesn’t change is human psychology. We value scarcity and as soon as an LLM can produce something an opening is created to define the new scarce resource (e.g. artisanal work or organic food).

As soon as AI can one-shot a task, we no longer consider it excellent or scarce. It becomes the new normal.

Excellent design is a constantly moving target and goes beyond expectations. Exploring the gap between average and excellent will remain the job of a skilled designer.

And that’s precisely why designers will not be replaced by AI.
