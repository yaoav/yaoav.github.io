We Paid for the Same Mistake 9 Times. So I Built My Construction Crew a Memory.

发表于2026-11-13|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @Kainative（大宇） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

I’m a contractor in small-town China. I can’t code. Here’s the AI system I built in 3 months to run my crew: every step, every mistake, and all 13 prompts.

Most teams don’t have a knowledge problem.

They have a memory problem.

The knowledge is there. It’s in the head of the guy who’s been doing this for twenty years. It’s in a conversation over dinner that nobody wrote down. It’s in a mistake someone made last week, at another site, that you’re about to make tomorrow.

I learned this the expensive way.

This summer I was going through my crew’s records, and I stopped cold.

5 project managers. 9 job sites. The same mistake, hit 9 separate times.

Nobody was lazy. Nobody was hiding anything. The first guy ran into it, came back, mentioned it to whoever was sitting next to him at dinner, and that was the end of it. The second guy went to a different site, ran into the exact same thing, and figured it out from scratch. Then the third. Then the fourth. It wasn’t until I was closing the books at the end of the month that I realized those 9 incidents were one problem.

We paid for the same lesson 9 times.

I run a construction crew in a small town in China. I can’t write code. I read blueprints.

So I made Claude Code my foreman and put Codex on the tools. Three-plus months of grinding later, I had an agent that helps me run the crew.

Before heading out, a project manager says one sentence: “Tomorrow, East Town site, bathroom seepage.”

It spits out a one-page pre-job brief. Similar jobs our crew has done before. The mistakes people made last time. What materials we still have in stock. Ready to print.

When the job’s done, he talks it through in three sentences. That becomes a lesson the whole crew can search the same day.

Once three similar lessons pile up, it finds the pattern and turns it into a checklist.

No internet on site? Doesn’t matter. Everyone carries a USB stick, and I merge everything once a week.

The part I’m proudest of: the whole thing runs without an AI model plugged in. You just lose a few lines of advice from the “old-timer.”

Part 1: What this actually is

In one sentence: it takes what’s in the old-timers’ heads and puts it on a single sheet of paper in a project manager’s hands before the job starts.

Long term, I want all of it boiled down to rules. Make the rules strict enough, and even a tiny model running locally, something like ByteDance’s Doubao, can keep a crew on track. More on that later. I think it’s the most valuable part of the whole thing.

There are six roles in the system. Each one stays in its lane.

  • Me (the boss): assigns work, makes the call, sets the rules, changes the rules. Doesn’t: write drafts or fill in forms.
  • Project managers: tick boxes on the checklist on site, say three sentences when the job’s done. Don’t: write anything about the parts that went smoothly.
  • Claude Code (the foreman): understands the big picture, proposes plans, writes rules, writes prompts. Doesn’t: change the rules directly. It can only propose.
  • Codex (the crew): writes code, runs batch jobs, carries out changes I’ve approved. Doesn’t: make judgment calls.
  • Rule-based code: recognizes place names, searches, ranks, copies checklists, keeps the books, levels people up, merges. Doesn’t: generate anything new.
  • The LLM: exactly four jobs. Clean up spoken notes, connect past experience, distill criteria, look things up online. Doesn’t: make calls or add facts.

My first attempt was a form. It failed.

My first idea was simple. A 21-field form that project managers fill out every day.

After a little over a month, I looked at the data and my heart sank.

In one batch, a single person contributed over 60% of the entries. Everyone else logged one or two a week. Safety records were around 5%. People love writing down what they solved today. Nobody loves writing down where something almost went wrong. And the killer: all of it sat in a folder. Not one record ever reached anyone before their next job.

I chewed on this for days. Then it clicked.

Doing the work is one thing. Filling out forms is another. As long as logging is separate from the work, it will shrink to the bare minimum. That isn’t anyone being lazy. That’s human nature. You can push people for a month. You can’t push them for a year.

So I flipped the whole thing around.

Before, the project managers worked for the system. Finish the job, then fill in the form. Now the system works for every job. Before the job, it gets you ready. After the job, it does the paperwork. Logging stops being an extra task and becomes something that falls out of the work on its own.

Every design decision after that came from this one idea.

  • No IT department → zero dependencies. Double-click a launcher and it works.
  • No internet on site, no shared network for the crew → everyone gets a USB stick, merged once a week.
  • Project managers won’t memorize commands → just talk normally. Anything that doesn’t look like a command gets answered as a question.
  • End-of-day notes are three to five words long → prompts built specifically for bare-bones spoken notes.
  • Tight budget → offline by default, the model only does four jobs, temperature kept low.
  • Names of people and sites can’t leave the building → code names only inside the system, and anything sent out gets scrubbed first.
  • On site, people want paper → pre-job briefs and safety cards download as Word files you can print.

Part 3: Building it

A quick note on the prompts: the originals are in Chinese, and these are faithful translations. A few rules depend on how Chinese works (character limits, matching on two-character chunks). Adjust those for your language.

Step 0: Get your tools together

You don’t need much.

Claude Code to think, plan, and write prompts. Codex to write code and run batch jobs. Any ordinary computer. Mine is a ThinkPad X1 I’ve had for years.

Then get an API key for an LLM. My main one is DeepSeek, with Alibaba’s Bailian platform as backup and for web search. Both use the OpenAI-compatible format, so if I want to switch to Doubao later, I just change the config.

Last, whatever old material you already have. Site logs, issue records, notes the old-timers scribbled, site photos. Any format. If you have it, throw it in.

Before anything else, set up your folders. Here’s my structure. Everything after this goes in here.

You don’t have to copy this. But rules, progress, proposals, tasks: I’d say you need those four. The next step explains why.

Step 1: Build the team. Get Claude and Codex working together.

I started out in the web chat.

I’d paste in my material, ask it to analyze, and the analysis looked pretty decent. Then I’d close the window, open it the next day, and it remembered nothing. I’d explain all over again what our crew does, how many people, where we left off. After the seventh or eighth time, I lost it.

There’s a sneakier problem, too. Talk to an AI long enough and it starts filling in the blanks on its own. Things you never said, it treats as things you said. An idea you floated in passing, it treats as a decision you made.

So I changed the approach: two AIs with split roles, and everything lives in files.

Claude thinks. Codex does. I only show up where a call needs making. They don’t hand off through chat. They hand off through files. That way you can swap in a different AI, it reads those few files, and it keeps going.

Here’s how I set it up.

First, write the rules. These are the ground rules for the whole system. Claude, Codex, or whatever AI comes next: the first thing they do when they walk in is read it.

Step 2: Pour the foundation. Turn experience into data.

I landed on three kinds of records.

A job. One file per job, one continuous thread from before you start to after you finish.

A lesson. A fixed set of 11 fields, coming up next. Each one gets a case ID like C-012.

A person. Trade × person, one big matrix. Every cell has four levels: never seen it, shadowed it, can do it solo, can lead others.

The 11 fields of a lesson:

  1. Site: always the full name
  2. Trade: pick from the list
  3. Location: e.g. bathroom floor, east exterior wall
  4. Material / spec: the field that ties together problems that run in a product line
  5. Problem category: pick from the list, no inventing
  6. Symptom: what you saw
  7. Cause: why it failed
  8. Fix: what you did
  9. Verification: how you confirmed it was fixed
  10. Open questions: what you still don’t understand
  11. Heads-up: one sentence the whole crew needs to know, or “none”

Once the fields are set, the first job is running all your old material through them.

I had a site log several hundred pages long, years of scraps written down here and there. Claude wrote the task spec, Codex broke it down stage by stage, and out came 133 lessons and 11 troubleshooting playbooks, sorted into 38 types of jobs.

Prompt 4: Breaking down old records (for Codex)

Step 3: The six-step loop. This is the backbone.

With the foundation poured, here’s the loop every single job runs through.

Look at that loop. It gets better on its own. Every lap adds a few lessons to the library, and the next pre-job brief for that kind of job gets a little thicker.

Loop 1: Say one sentence, get a pre-job brief

Before heading out, a project manager says: “Tomorrow, East Town site, bathroom seepage.”

The system gives back one page in four sections. Similar jobs our crew has done, with sources. An old-timer’s briefing, written by the model, kept to a few short sentences. Outside references, looked up only if you explicitly ask it to go online, and marked as unverified. And last, what materials we have in stock.

Recognition and search are all rules.

Pulling the site, the problem, and the people out of one sentence: rules. For site names, the order is: check the known-sites list first, then look for a place name after words like “go to,” “at,” or “in,” and only then check word endings. Search runs on a synonym table plus fuzzy matching on two-character chunks, so if a PM says “damp,” it still finds “seepage.”

If the problem is too vague, like just “leaking,” it doesn’t rush out a brief. It asks back first: bathroom, roof, exterior wall, or pipes?

The model does exactly one thing at the end: turn the search results into a few lines of briefing.

Prompt 5: The old-timer’s briefing (connecting experience)

Prompt 6: Recognition and search rules (for Codex, pure rules, no model)

Loop 2: Print a safety card and put it in your pocket

Pull three sections out of the checklist: the pre-start check, hazards and prevention, and the known problems. Problems tied to this site get a star and go to the top.

Why this step? A lot of places on site have no signal, and while you’re working there’s no time to pull out a phone. A sheet of paper in your pocket beats everything.

I don’t let the model touch this step at all.

The rule-based code pulls the three sections by heading and copies them word for word. Not one word changed. Known problems get copied along with the finder’s code name. If there’s no checklist for that kind of job yet, it says so straight out. It won’t make one up.

Prompt 7: Safety card extraction (for Codex, no model)

Loop 3: On site, only log what differs from the checklist

Tick through the checklist step by step. Smooth parts, tick and move on.

You only pick up a pen in three cases.

Deviation: reality differs from the checklist. A step changed, time ran over, the drawings don’t match the site.

First time: someone did something on their own for the first time, or someone got stuck on a step.

Danger: a hazard point acted up, or something almost went wrong.

I set myself a hard rule: nobody writes for more than 5 minutes a day. Anything that can be inferred from the ticks, nobody gets asked to write.

So now it’s photos first, then talk. Photos taken within 30 minutes get attached to that day’s review automatically. When the picture matches the story, it’s clearer than any written description.

Loop 4: Three end-of-day questions, filed automatically

On the day the job wraps, or at the meeting the next morning, the lead talks and the trainee writes.

Three questions.

What was different from the pre-job brief?

Did you run into anything new, and how did you handle it?

Who got stuck, and who did something on their own for the first time?

Having the trainee do the writing is deliberate. Writing it down is learning. And it fixes the problem of one person doing all the logging.

End-of-day template (print-ready)

Prompt 8: Review filing (spoken note → fields)

Loop 5: Three of a kind. Distill the criteria, lock them into a checklist

Say “distill: bathroom seepage,” and the system pulls every similar case from the database and distills 3 to 5 shared criteria. Each one is tagged with the cases it came from.

Then say “lock in: bathroom seepage,” and on top of those criteria it drafts a checklist in five fixed sections: scope, decision criteria, work steps, risk prevention, mandatory checkpoints. The draft goes into a pending area first. It only counts after I’ve read it and signed off.

Why bother? Lessons are bricks. Criteria and checklists are the wall. A pile of bricks doesn’t keep the wind out.

There are several gates on this step, all run by rules.

Fewer than 3 similar cases, it refuses outright: “There are only 2 in the database. Come back when there are more.” Two cases don’t make a pattern. Forcing one out is just paraphrasing.

Every criterion the model distills has to end with the cases it’s based on, like (based on: C-012, C-047).

The rule-based code checks those IDs one by one. If a cited ID doesn’t exist in the material, the whole criterion gets thrown out.

The list of source cases at the end is assembled by the code itself. It never passes through the model’s hands.

If no model is configured, or the call fails, it falls back to a compilation: the original text of the similar cases, laid out neatly. The feature doesn’t break, and it never makes anything up.

Prompt 9: Distilling criteria

Prompt 10: Locking in a checklist draft

Loop 6: Weekly merge, shipped back on USB

Everyone has a USB stick and runs the system on their own computer. Each week they hand their stuff to me, I merge it, pack a new data bundle, and send it back to everyone.

Small-town job sites have no shared network. This was the most low-tech, most reliable method I could think of.

Here’s how it works.

Day to day, reviews don’t write to the main database. They go into each person’s own “new this week” file. Search reads the main database and this week’s entries together, so a PM can find a lesson he logged the same day.

On the weekend I say “merge.” The system produces a weekly report, folds everyone’s new entries into the main database, renumbers everything, writes the week’s level-ups into the skills matrix, bumps the version number, and packs a new bundle.

I send the bundle back. Everyone drags it into their sync folder. The whole crew is in sync.

Prompt 11: Weekly merge and distribution bundle (for Codex)

Step 4: A cheat sheet of mistakes. Save this one.

I’ve scattered a lot of mistakes above. Here they are in one place. The symptom you’ll hit sooner or later, why it happens, and what I did about it.

  • Commands had to be typed exactly; one extra character and they failed. Matching too strict. → If the keyword appears, it counts. Anything that doesn’t look like a command gets answered as a question.
  • Acronyms in the interface nobody understood. I brought my own jargon in. → Plain language everywhere. “AAR” became “the three end-of-day questions.”
  • New users opened it and didn’t know where to click. I knew it too well to notice. → Have Claude walk through it as a newcomer (Prompt 12).
  • Material dragged in couldn’t be found later. Only the text was extracted, the original wasn’t saved, and there was no way in from the interface. → Save originals to disk, add a “My files” entry.
  • The model invented field values. Temperature too high. → Filing 0.15, connecting 0.25. Only brainstorming goes up to 1.0.
  • The order of steps got reversed. High temperature, and the prompt didn’t stress it. → Lower temperature, and the prompt explicitly forbids reversing order.
  • The model read its rules out loud. Constraints treated as content. → Code cuts those sentences.
  • Cited case IDs that don’t exist. The model padding the format. → Code checks IDs; fake ones get dropped whole.
  • Patterns forced out of two cases. No threshold. → Fewer than 3, refuse.
  • Safety cards stuffed with generic filler. Let the model tidy it up. → No model in this step. Copy the original.
  • Bare-bones notes came back with every field empty. Prompt too cautious. → Slot in by order, with an example.
  • First trip to a new site, recognition failed. Only knew sites in the database. → Rule for place names after “go to” / “at.”
  • Locations taken as sites. No exclusion list. → Add a location exclusion list.
  • Another site tagged as this site. It was mentioned in the body. → Only the site name in the title counts.
  • One site, many names. Casual speech. → Standardize to the database’s full name at filing.
  • One character misfiled a whole batch. Rule too loose. → Drop the single character, reorder, rerun everything.
  • USB ID collisions, forked main database. Everyone writing their own copy. → Keep this week’s entries separate from the main database; renumber at merge.
  • Real names almost got packed. No check. → Automatic pre-pack check, and I confirm it myself.

Prompt 12: Newcomer walkthrough (for Claude)

Step 5: From agent to rules. Less compute the more you use it.

Everything so far is the how. This part is about the thing I care about most.

Looking back on these three-plus months, I actually went through four stages.

  1. Web chat. Paste material in, have the AI analyze it. The model does: everything, and forgets it all when the chat ends.
  2. Skills. Scripts handle what can be calculated: stats, clustering, alerts. The model just polishes wording. The model does: about half.
  3. Agent. Rule-based code runs the main flow. The model is down to four jobs. The model does: very little.
  4. Rules (in progress). Keep peeling out whatever in those four jobs can become a rule. Hand the rest to a small local model. The model does: the least.

With each stage, it costs a bit less, the output gets a bit more stable, and it leans on the model a bit less.

By stage four, a small local model is enough. The data never even has to leave the crew’s computers.

For a small-town crew like ours, this matters enormously. No servers, no IT, and the budget is what it is. If a management system can’t run without the most expensive model, it simply won’t survive here.

So how do you decide whether a step can go to rules? I boiled it down to three questions.

Is the input format fixed? Like checklist headings, or the end-of-day table.

Can the judgment be written as “if X, do Y”? Like “fewer than 3, refuse.”

Is a mistake expensive? Like a wrong safety card, which on site means an accident.

Three yeses, and it becomes a rule. Only what needs to understand human speech or summarize wording stays with the model.

Keep temperature low. Filing 0.15, connecting 0.25.

Put examples in the prompt. “Rebar spacing wrong, re-tied, re-inspection passed.” That one example does more than ten rules.

Make the model output JSON and let code check it. Are the fields right? Are the IDs real? Let code judge. Don’t count on the model to police itself.

When it fails, fall back. Model not configured, call failed, output didn’t pass: always fall back to listing the original text. The feature doesn’t break, and it never makes things up.

Right now, my agent opens fine with no API key at all. Site recognition, search, the first section of the pre-job brief, safety cards, end-of-day bookkeeping, level-ups, the weekly merge: all of it works as normal. All you lose is the old-timer’s few lines of briefing and the criteria distilling.

Switching models is a config change. The engine only speaks the OpenAI-compatible format. Endpoint, model name, and key all live in the config file. If I want to plug in Doubao, or a locally hosted model, I change those three things. Not one line of code.

Prompt 13: Rules audit (for Claude)

Step 6: A few boundaries

Last, the lines I’ve held the whole time.

AI drafts, people decide. Checklists, criteria, skill level-ups: every one of them needs a human nod.

Names of people and sites never go online. Everyone in the system is a code name. Anything sent to an outside model gets its code names and site names swapped out first, and place names not in the database get masked.

Points can be ranked, but not gamed. I changed my mind on this one partway through. At first I refused to rank anyone. I was worried people would log junk for points. Then I found that a little friendly comparison actually made people more willing to log. Now points only come from real records: filing one case is 2 points, filling in all four sections is +1, a level-up is 3. Level-ups only count with proof, so there’s nothing to game.

Final words

This thing is still evolving. Next, I plan to clean up a few of the skills and open-source them.

Looking back on these three-plus months, the hardest part wasn’t the AI at all.

AI writes code ten thousand times faster than me. It writes tidier prompts than I do, too. The hard part was figuring out which things to hand to it and which calls I have to make myself. Then writing those decisions down, one by one, as rules it can follow.

That job can only be done by someone who’s worked a site.

That 5-people-9-sites mistake from the beginning is now nailed onto a checklist. Last month, one of our project managers went to a new site and ran into the exact same situation.

Before he left, it was already on his sheet.

AI doesn’t make my calls.

It makes sure that when I make them, I have the right information in my hands.

P.S. I’m a second-generation contractor, and I’m out to be the best contractor in my county.
