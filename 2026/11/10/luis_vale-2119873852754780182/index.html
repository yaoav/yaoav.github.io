Grok bot for GTM: how I built a team you can copy

发表于2026-11-10|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @luis_vale（Luis Vale） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

Last week, I had a launch go viral on X.

If you haven’t seen it, @maryamyy0 and I launched Sexbot, a fake robot company. We made a launch video and played the whole thing straight. This is the post:

That led to what people would call good problems. I was absolutely capacity constrained: doing outreach, writing more social media posts, building my product, and demoing it to the new influx of users.

Luckily I’m building @trydreamstate, a product working on automating all things growth. Finding leads, writing content, doing outreach and keeping track of replies. So I have a pretty good idea of how to build systems like this. It’s what I’m building the product for.

I’ve spent this past week trying to automate everything I do, and Grok @bot has been a huge unlock for me.

I liked @kristaletz’s articles on how she uses Grok Bot for GTM and how her team shares specialist bots. I wanted to share our version, including the instructions and skills, so you can actually copy it.

The repo is public. Here’s how I’ve split up the work and how the bots learn from what happens after.

The two parts of the team

I’ve split it into socials and outbound. The Head of Content looks after the writers and the designer. The Outbound Lead looks after finding the right people and preparing the outreach. The Chief of Staff sits above both, so I have somewhere to take a goal that needs the whole team.

The Content Room is their shared chat, not another bot. Grok Bot holds the team and conversations. Dreamstate holds the drafts, lead tables, sequences and inbox. File work goes through the Chief of Staff to a coding agent.

Socials: same idea, different writers

I want to be able to send one idea to the team without getting the same post back with different line breaks.

They all need to know what Dreamstate does and what I can honestly claim. That comes from the same Company Context. But each writer also needs its own brief: who it’s writing for, what this piece is meant to do, examples I’ve approved for that channel and the skill for that kind of writing.

The X Writer has instructions for single posts, threads and Articles. If an idea fits in a post, it stays a post. A thread needs actual steps or a story that develops. An Article like this one gets room to show the whole setup, instead of a short post stretched out to fill the space.

The LinkedIn Writer works from my LinkedIn examples and a specific aim, like reach, belief or signups. It chooses a story, an argument or a how-to from the material I gave it. The opening needs to make someone want to keep reading, and the close needs to follow from the post. It isn’t the X draft with a more serious first sentence.

The YouTube Writer has a different job again. It packages a real video with title options, a thumbnail brief, a description and chapters from the transcript. The Reddit Writer first reads the particular subreddit’s rules and posts, then picks a shape like a behind-the-scenes story or a guide. Another subreddit gets another rewrite.

For a small illustration, not posts I’ve published, take the idea that attention after a launch isn’t the same as buyer interest.

On X, I might make the point directly: “Someone liking my launch post isn’t the same as wanting a demo. I want the bots to check the fit before they write the message.”

For LinkedIn, I could explain the decision: “After a launch, the follow-up can become another job. I’m building a workflow that keeps the person’s question and the company fit next to the post they came from. I want to see that context before I review the message. How do you decide which conversations to follow up on?”

Same idea. One is a short claim. The other explains a work problem and the decision I’m making, then asks how other people handle it. The facts shouldn’t change between them, but the writing should.

What each writer keeps learning

I still want all of it to sound like me. I just don’t want one rule for an X opening to turn into a rule for every video description and Reddit post.

The shared voice guide is the starting point. What I want each writer to build up around it is a set of channel-specific examples and corrections. If I tell the X Writer an opening assumes people know who I am, keep that note with the version I edited and the opening I accepted. Use it on the next X draft. Don’t leave it buried in this chat.

Then the Head of Content can look at my feedback alongside that platform’s results: which post, what angle, what opening, how old the numbers are and what people actually said. Compare my X posts with my other X posts, and LinkedIn with LinkedIn. A post I liked writing and a post that started useful conversations are both worth understanding.

The useful changes go back into that writer’s examples, brief or skill, with the reason behind them. If a correction applies everywhere, it can go into the shared guide. If it’s specific to a channel, keep it there. I review the changes rather than having a good-looking number silently rewrite everyone’s instructions.

There’s also shared source material, so the writers don’t all have to research the same thing again.

Before we made Sexbot, we used the Dreamstate API and a coding agent to pull launch posts into a workbook. We looked at the hooks, the video lengths and the reported results. That became our public launch video library.

Launch Video owns that research and the video plan. The writers can use the same evidence for their own pieces, and the Graphic Designer gets the accepted angle and a visual reference.

For this article, I sent a thumbnail reference and asked for “Grok for GTM” in white with our colors. The first options were too flat, so I asked for the gradient and texture from the reference.

I didn’t need the writer to become a designer. I needed it to pass along what I asked for to the designer bot and then bring the options back.

Outbound: start with people already paying attention

This is the part I want connected to socials. If someone reacts to one of my posts or asks a useful question, I don’t want that context to disappear while a different bot goes off looking for a cold list.

I want to start with those people. The Lead Sourcer can put the engagement into a table with the post link, what the person did, when they did it and what we know about them. A recent comment gives us more to work with than an old like. Someone coming back to ask another question is different from a one-off reaction.

Then check whether they fit the product. Are they at a company we can help? Does their role or their question give us a real reason to think this is relevant? Find their LinkedIn profile and work email where available, and make sure it’s actually the same person. Missing information stays missing.

A like puts someone on a list to check. It doesn’t mean they want a message, and it doesn’t make them a buyer. I review the qualified people and the outreach before anything goes out.

The context needs to survive that handoff. If someone asks about keeping track of replies after a launch, and their company fits, I want the Outbound Copywriter to have that actual question in front of it. It can write about the problem they raised instead of inventing a personal opener. “Thanks for liking my post” followed by a random pitch isn’t what I’m trying to build.

Give outbound its own tests

Socials is learning what I can write about and how to explain it. Outbound has a different set of questions: am I finding the right people, is the offer relevant, and does the message give them a reason to reply?

For example, I could have the Outbound Copywriter prepare two openings for the same qualified group, with the same offer and channel. One leads with the problem the person raised. The other leads with a concrete thing we can help them do. Keep the rest similar enough that we’re actually testing the opening.

Another test could be the audience: people who have asked a relevant question versus people who only reacted, after checking both groups against the same customer definition. That tells us something different from a copy test. I’d keep those results separate instead of changing the audience, offer and message at once and guessing what mattered.

Those are examples of tests I want to run, not campaign results I’m claiming here.

For each test, keep the campaign, source post, qualification reason and message version together. Then look at what was sent and what came back over the same date window. Dreamstate has sequence analytics and a step-by-step variant scoreboard for that. Its overall reply rate is replies per message or invitation, not the percentage of people who became customers.

And I care about the replies themselves. A higher reply rate made up of “not relevant” isn’t the result I want. The Outbound Lead needs to read that alongside lead quality and delivery problems, then bring back a specific change: tighten the filter, change the offer or try different wording.

If the same objection keeps coming up, that can change the next brief and the copy skill. If the wrong people keep qualifying, change the sourcing rule and keep the examples that show why. The next run should start from those reviewed instructions, not repeat the same test because nobody saved what we learned.

Once there’s a real conversation, Reply Manager owns the reply drafts. Quiet deals go to Deal Reactivation. Someone already talking to me shouldn’t get dropped into another first-touch sequence because they liked a new post.

How I want the two parts to work together

I post something. Someone relevant engages. The Lead Sourcer checks the fit and keeps the context. The outbound team prepares a message about a problem we can actually help with. Then the replies give us something useful to bring back to both teams.

If people keep asking the same question in replies, the Head of Content has an idea worth explaining properly. If a post attracts people who don’t fit, I want to know whether the angle was too broad or whether the sourcing rule was wrong. The answer might be a better post, a better filter or both.

That’s why I want the work in one system. I don’t want to explain the launch to the writer, explain it again to the sourcing bot, then explain the same person’s question to whoever writes the outreach. Keep the source post, the lead and the conversation connected, and use what we learn on the next attempt.

That’s the setup I’m building toward with these roles and tools. It still needs real results and my feedback to tell us what to change. More likes don’t automatically mean better leads, and logging a reply doesn’t update a skill by itself.

If you want to copy it, start with the setup prompt, add your own company context and give it one real idea. Ask for separate X and LinkedIn drafts, then a warm-lead handoff brief that explains who would be relevant and why. Drafts only.

Then read what comes back, tell it what’s wrong and make sure the next run doesn’t need the same explanation again.
