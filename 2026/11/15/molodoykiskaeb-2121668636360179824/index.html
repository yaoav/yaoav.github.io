Harness, Loops and Graphs for Everyone: The Only Guide You Need

8 viral guides, 20 million views, one thing in common: they were all written for programmers. Here is everything they agree on, in plain English, with no code

In August, a guide called “Harness Engineering” got 10.5 million views

Then came the loops. Then the graphs

In under three months, the 8 biggest guides on the topic passed 20 million views

Your feed is full of them

And every single one was written for programmers

Bug reports. Pull requests. Test suites. Python files you’re supposed to paste into a terminal

Which is strange, because the problem they describe is not a programmer problem

It’s yours

You ask an AI to do something. It says “done”. You check. It isn’t

You explain what’s wrong. It fixes that and breaks something else

Next week it makes the same mistake again

That’s not a bad model. That’s a missing harness

So I read all 8 guides, plus the Anthropic, OpenAI and Google research they’re built on

90% of them say the same thing

Here is that 90% in plain English. And the 10% they still argue about

  • 4 fights the guides still haven’t settled

  • Step 0: one message that gives any AI a harness

One example runs through all of it: an AI that handles your inbox. No code anywhere. If a step needs a terminal, it’s not in this article

The 30-second map

A harness gives your AI a desk: a job description, the tools it needs, notes it doesn’t lose, and doors it can’t open

A loop gives it an exam it can fail, and makes it retake the exam until it passes

A graph gives a team of AIs an org chart: who does what, in what order, and who signs off

That’s it. Everything else in those 20 million views is detail

Level 0: you are the harness

You paste an email into a chat. The AI writes a reply

You read it, fix two lines, copy it into Gmail and press send

Look at who did what:

The AI wrote one paragraph. You did everything else

Mitchell Hashimoto, who created Terraform, says a generic agent harness beats every built-in “ask me anything” chat box he has tried

The model was never the problem. The empty room around it was

The 7 rules every guide agrees on

8 guides. Different authors, different examples, different tools

I counted how many of them state each rule or clearly build around it. The counts are mine

Rule 1: Write “done” before it starts (8 of 8)

“Answer my emails” is a wish

An AI given a wish will quietly finish an easier job and call it done

Every guide starts the same way: before the AI touches anything, write down what finished looks like

If you can’t write this list, you don’t know what you want yet. Neither does your AI

Rule 2: A rule in a text box is a wish. A setting is a wall (8 of 8)

“Please don’t send anything without me” is a sentence

Your AI will follow it, until the day it doesn’t

A setting it can’t change is a wall

Every AI employee already has walls. Most people never open the settings:

  • Claude Duet always shows you the exact action and waits before it sends, pays, signs or deletes. Purchases go through a one-time card
  • ChatGPT dots: turn your most important line into a custom rule set to “ask first”, so it holds even on the day you forget to say it
  • Grok Bot: add your own “Ask first” rules on top of Auto Review. And leave “Securely save to Bot’s vault” unchecked if you want to approve every payment
  • Any AI connected to your email: if it only needs to read, don’t give it the right to send

If you want to see what happens when an agent decides a rule doesn’t apply to it, my first article is about two that turned off their own alarms ↓

Rule 3: Memory lives outside the chat (8 of 8)

The chat is short-term memory. It fills up, gets summarized, and loses things

Anthropic ran into this when its agents worked for hours across many sessions. The fix they published was boring, and it worked: a progress note the agent reads at the start and updates at the end

For your inbox, the note looks like this:

“Tried” is the line that saves you the most

Without it, your AI forgets what didn’t work and walks straight back into it

Rule 4: The one who did the work never grades it (8 of 8)

Ask an AI if its own work is good, and it will say yes

Anthropic found exactly that: agents grading their own work kept praising it, even when a person could see it was mediocre

So the check has to come from somewhere else: a checklist, a real test, or a second chat that only judges and can’t fix anything

And “checked” means checked for real. Boris Cherny, who created Claude Code, made it one of his five tips for letting Claude work for hours: give it a way to check the result end to end, not just say it looks fine

Rule 5: Feedback, not a verdict (7 of 8)

“That’s wrong, try again” is a verdict

It tells your AI almost nothing, so it guesses

Real feedback has three lines:

Expected and saw turn “wrong” into a direction

Tried stops attempt three from quietly becoming attempt one again

Rule 6: The same result twice means stop (8 of 8)

If your AI gives you the same wrong answer twice, the third try won’t fix it

It means it can’t see something it needs, or you asked the wrong question

Every guide gives the work three exits:

A loop with no exit isn’t persistence

It’s an open tab on your card

Rule 7: You sign only what can’t be undone (8 of 8)

You are the slowest and most expensive part of the whole system

So spend yourself where a mistake can’t be taken back, and nowhere else. @m4lkxx calls it gating on the price of undo

The last row has no confidence score. Not 95%, not 99%

There is no number for the AI to beat, so there is nothing to argue with

Bonus: the same correction twice becomes a rule (6 of 8)

Every time you fix the same thing twice, write it into the notes as a rule

It’s the same rule the Grok Bot team runs on. Rule 6 in my breakdown of all 18 of their guides ↓

The test is simple: the same mistake never costs you twice

Graphs, without the jargon

One AI with a good desk and a real exam will handle most of your inbox

A graph is for the day one AI isn’t enough

It’s an org chart: who does what, in what order, and who signs off

Three rules for the org chart:

  • One job per AI. A sorter that also writes forgets which job it’s doing
  • Separate desks. If the checker can see how the writer was thinking, it agrees with the writer. Give it only the email and the draft
  • Nothing waits for no reason. If two jobs don’t need each other’s result, run them at the same time. Most “first this, then that” is just the order you happened to think of them

And the most repeated advice about graphs: don’t build one on day one (7 of 8)

Get one AI working with a desk and an exam first. Add the org chart when the work itself splits into pieces

Which level are you on?

Find the first symptom from the top. That’s your next step. Not the one after it

Where the guides disagree

The 7 rules are the 90%. Here is the 10% they still fight about, and where I land on each

Fight 1: Is an AI reviewer worth it?

Anthropic built its long-running setup around a separate AI judge

Lauren Tan, who works on Grok Bot at SpaceXAI, calls adversarial AI reviews “mostly a waste of tokens”

Both are right, about different reviewers

A judge asked “is this good?” gives you opinions

A judge asked one narrow question, “does this draft pass each line of the checklist?”, gives you evidence

My take: use the checker with one narrow question. Never as your only gate

Fight 2: More AIs, better or worse?

Cognition, the team behind Devin, published “Don’t Build Multi-Agents”

One day later, Anthropic published how it built its multi-agent research system

Then Google and MIT tested 180 setups. Depending on the task, more AIs gave anywhere from +81% to -70%

When the work split into independent pieces, a coordinator with helpers won big. On step-by-step planning, every multi-agent setup got worse

My take: more AIs help only when the work splits cleanly. A full inbox splits. Planning your week doesn’t

Fight 3: Should your AI ask before every step?

Boris Cherny’s first tip for long runs: auto mode, so Claude stops asking you for approval

Every guide also says: keep a human in front of anything risky

Both are true, on different rows of the undo table

My take: let it run free on drafts and notes. Never on sends, payments and deletes

Fight 4: The org chart first or last?

Most guides: draw it last, only when one AI clearly isn’t enough

@0xarcos builds everything around it. He says his 20 agents shipped 143 changes in one night, and he approved 6

The difference is the work. He had big jobs that split cleanly, and real tests to check every piece

My take: start with one AI. Draw the org chart when your work, not your excitement, splits into pieces

The numbers that never make the headline

  • AIs passing work to each other with nobody checking in between multiplied errors 17.2 times. With a coordinator checking in the middle: 4.4 times (Google and MIT)
  • If one AI already gets a task right about 45% of the time, adding more AIs often makes it worse (Google and MIT)
  • Multi-agent setups burn about 15 times the tokens of a normal chat (Anthropic)
  • One AI alone: 20 minutes, $9, a game that didn’t work. The same request with a planner, a builder and a separate judge: 6 hours, $200, a game that worked (Anthropic)

$9 for a broken game isn’t cheaper

It’s $9 for nothing

A harness isn’t a way to spend more. It’s how you stop paying for junk

Step 0: one message that gives any AI a harness

Where it goes:

  • Claude or ChatGPT: make a Project and paste it into the project instructions. A chat can’t save files on its own, so at the end of each chat, copy the updated notes into the project yourself. 30 seconds
  • Grok Bot: make a new Bot and send this as its first message
  • Claude Duet or ChatGPT dots: paste it, then turn the “Never” line into a real setting (Rule 2)

The same harness for 3 other jobs

Your inbox was just the example. The harness doesn’t care what the job is

Swap these into the brackets of Step 0. Everything else in the message stays the same

1. Posts for your social media

The usual failure: it sounds like a robot, invents a number, and you rewrite every line

2. A weekly brief on your topic

The usual failure: a confident summary of something it didn’t actually read

3. Invoices you’re still waiting on

The usual failure: it sends a reminder to a client who already paid, and you apologize

Notice what didn’t change: a finish line, a wall, a note, a separate checker, and you on the button that can’t be undone

5 copy-paste blocks

2. Feedback that works. Use it every time you correct your AI

3. The stop rule

4. The weekly lesson. Turns your corrections into rules

5. The front door. For the day you add a second AI

If you write code

Everything above works in Claude Code too, and there the walls are real: permission rules, a stop check that won’t let Claude finish until the checklist passes, /goal and /loop

For the deep version, start with the two guides embedded above: @m4lkxx’s 7-level ladder and @0xarcos’s 20 agents overnight, file by file

Your first week

  1. Day 1. Paste Step 0 into one AI. One job only: today’s inbox. 10 minutes
  2. Day 2. Watch it work once. Write down every correction you make
  3. Day 3. Open the settings. Turn your “Never” line into a real wall
  4. Day 4. Add the checker in a separate chat
  5. Day 5. Add the stop rule. Use the feedback block every time you correct it
  6. Day 6. Read your working notes. Is “Tried” filling up? Good
  7. Day 7. Run the weekly lesson. Add a second AI only if the first one does its job without you

The short version

8 guides. 20 million views. Thousands of lines of code

Under all of it, one idea:

Stop being your AI’s harness

Give it a desk. Give it an exam it can fail. Give it a way to stop

The desk makes it useful. The exam makes it honest. The org chart makes it fast

And you stay where you belong: on the one button that can’t be undone
