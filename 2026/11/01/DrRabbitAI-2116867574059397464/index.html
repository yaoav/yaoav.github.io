How to Write a Research Paper Introduction

发表于2026-11-01|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @DrRabbitAI（Roxy） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

A method drawn from the classic literature, plus the AI prompts I use

Lately I keep hearing students at my university talk about the same thing: how do you actually write the introduction of a research paper?

So I went back to the sources: John Swales’ CARS model, the classic framework behind almost every introduction template; “Ten simple rules for structuring papers” in PLOS Computational Biology; and the author guidelines from Springer Nature, Elsevier and the ACS. From them I put together a systematic method for writing introductions. I’m also sharing the prompts I personally use when I work on introductions with AI.

One thing I always come back to: in academic research, the question is not whether to use AI, but how to use it properly.

This post has two parts:

  • Part 1: how to write an introduction
  • Part 2: the AI prompts I find most useful

First, here is what a typical student draft looks like:

Short-video platforms have grown rapidly in recent years and attracted wide academic attention. Studies have examined short-video use in relation to academic performance, sleep quality, and mental health. Research has also found that recommendation algorithms shape what users see. Therefore, this study surveyed college students’ short-video use.

Nothing in it is wrong. But a supervisor will immediately ask: so what problem are you actually solving? Part 1 explains why, and how to fix it.

Part 1: How to write the introduction

1. An introduction does only three things

Most advice about introductions, from the “funnel” to the “four-paragraph template”, is a simplified version of one model: CARS (Create a Research Space), proposed by linguist John Swales in 1990. After analysing published research articles, Swales found that introductions do three things:

  • Move 1: Establish a territory. Why does this area matter, and what has already been done?
  • Move 2: Establish a niche. What is still unresolved?
  • Move 3: Occupy the niche. What does this study do about it?

Put simply: map the field, dig the gap, fill the gap.

Look at the draft above again. It has a territory (short videos are popular) and a study (we surveyed students), but no gap. We learn what earlier studies examined, but not what they left unanswered. The study appears out of nowhere.

This is not unusual. A comparison of English and Chinese research article introductions in educational psychology found that the biggest difference between the two lay precisely in Move 2, establishing the niche (Loi & Evans, 2010). If you practise one thing, practise digging the gap.

2. Paragraph by paragraph: from known to unknown

“Ten simple rules for structuring papers” (Mensh & Kording, 2017, PLOS Computational Biology) gives three rules for the introduction:

  • Narrow progressively. Move from the broad field to the specific question.
  • Go from known to unknown in each paragraph. State what is known, then end on what is not.
  • Use the last paragraph to say how this paper fills the gap.

A common introduction has four paragraphs:

Paragraph 1: Why this area matters (Territory)

Paragraph 2: What previous work has achieved (Territory)

Paragraph 3: What remains unresolved (Niche)

Paragraph 4: What this study does (Occupy)

The number of paragraphs is not fixed. In a complex field, paragraphs 2 and 3 can repeat: one strand of work, one limitation; another strand, another limitation.

3. Four ways to dig the gap

Digging the gap is the hardest part. Swales identified four ways to do it:

  1. Indicate a gap: nobody has studied X. “However, little is known about…”
  2. Counter-claim: previous findings have limits. “These findings, however, are based on…”
  3. Raise a question: previous work leaves a question open. “It remains unclear whether…”
  4. Continue a tradition: previous work started something; take it one step further. “Building on…, this study extends…”

For more sentence patterns, the University of Manchester’s free Academic Phrasebank organises its “Introducing work” section by these same functions. Use the phrases as models, not as paragraphs to paste.

Two warnings:

Don’t demolish previous work. One line on what others did, followed by a list of their flaws, is the pattern reviewers dislike most, and your reviewer may well be one of the authors you are criticising. Acknowledge how far previous work has come, then name the one problem that matters for your study.

Be careful with “no study has” and “for the first time”. One counterexample from a reviewer and the claim collapses. An editor’s guidance on Elsevier Connect also advises restraint with “novel” and “first time”. Limit the claim to what you actually searched: “A search of Web of Science for 2015–2025 found no study that…”

4. The last paragraph

The final paragraph states your aim, and the aim must answer the gap you dug. If you said previous work has not examined X’s effect on Y, your aim should be to examine X’s effect on Y, not some other question.

Should you also preview your main findings? Sources disagree. Mensh & Kording say the last paragraph should summarise how the gap is filled, and Drake & Han (2025) allow a brief “Here we show…”. Springer Nature’s author guidance and the Elsevier editor recommend ending on a clear aim and keeping results for later. My advice: read the last paragraph of 5–10 recent papers in your target journal and follow what they do.

5. Three common mistakes

Writing a literature review. Springer Nature’s guidance puts it bluntly: “Do not write a literature review in your Introduction.” The ACS Style Guide likewise warns against a general survey of semi-relevant literature. Every citation in an introduction should serve the gap. If it doesn’t, cut it.

Too much background. The Elsevier editor suggests roughly 1.5–2 pages and warns against turning the introduction into a history lesson.

Writing the introduction first. Both the Elsevier editor and Drake & Han (2025) recommend writing the methods and results first and the introduction last. Only once you have results do you know which gap you have actually filled.

A note for social scientists: papers in the social sciences often give the literature review, theoretical framework and hypotheses their own sections, so the introduction itself is shorter and focuses on the gap and the contribution. Check how papers in your target journal are structured before you start.

7. A five-question check before you submit

  1. Read only the first sentence of each paragraph. Do they form a mini-introduction?
  2. Can you state, in one sentence, the gap you dug?
  3. Is that gap supported by the literature, or did you invent it?
  4. Does your aim answer exactly that gap?
  5. Is there a block of literature review that has nothing to do with the gap?

Part 2: Using AI to speed things up

First, a conclusion: don’t ask AI to write your introduction from scratch.

The most important part of an introduction is its references, and references are what AI fabricates most easily. In a 2023 study in Scientific Reports, 55% of the references produced by GPT-3.5 and 18% of those produced by GPT-4 were fabricated (Walters & Wilder, 2023).

AI-written introductions also love one particular opening: “In recent years, X has attracted increasing attention.” Reviewers have seen it so often that they spot it instantly.

So use AI to find the gap, build the outline, draft, check, and polish, while you supply the literature yourself. Here are the five prompts I use, in the order I use them. Try them and see whether they work for you.

Prompt 1: Find the gap in papers you have read

I am writing the introduction of a paper. Below are my research question, main results, and abstracts of the key papers I have read.
Answer using only the material I provide. Do not add other literature:

  1. Summarise what previous work has achieved, citing the paper for each point;
  2. List 3 problems previous work has not solved (research gaps), and say which papers support each gap;
  3. Decide which gap best matches my results, and explain why.
    Do not write the introduction yet.
    [Research question]
    [Main results]
    [Abstracts] (author and year for each)

The key is the first instruction: only the material I provide. That way every gap the AI names can be traced to a specific paper.

Here is what it looks like filled in, in YILA, the AI research tool I use (the four papers are real; the research question and results are illustrative). The full filled-in version is at the end of this post.

A tip on tokens: if you run these prompts in Codex, don’t use the Astra model; if you use YILA, don’t use Expert mode. Both burn through tokens fast, especially when you upload a lot of papers.

Prompt 2: Outline each paragraph

Using the CARS model (establish a territory, establish a niche, occupy the niche), outline a 4-paragraph introduction.
Requirements:

  1. One sentence per paragraph, saying what the paragraph must do;
  2. Mark which papers from my material each paragraph should cite;
  3. Use the research gap I have confirmed: [paste];
  4. The research aim must directly answer this gap.
    Do not write the text. Do not add literature I have not provided.
    [Abstracts]

This follows Drake & Han’s method: write one purpose sentence per paragraph first, and delete those sentences once the text is written. Confirm the outline before moving on.

Prompt 3: Draft from the outline

Write a first draft of the introduction in English, following the outline below.
Requirements:

  1. Cite only the papers I provide, as (Author, Year);
  2. If a sentence has no supporting paper, mark it [citation needed]. Do not invent one;
  3. Do not open with “In recent years” or “has attracted increasing attention”;
  4. Do not use “for the first time” or “novel”;
  5. Do not add study limitations that are not in my material.
    [Outline]
    [Abstracts]

Requirement 2 matters most: leave it blank rather than make it up. Requirement 5 targets AI’s “defensive writing”: its habit of adding “may” and “further research is needed” everywhere.

When I ran this prompt, the one claim my four papers could not support was marked [citation needed] instead of being given an invented reference.

Prompt 4: Check, don’t rewrite

Check the introduction below. Do not rewrite it; only list problems.

  1. For each paragraph, say whether it establishes a territory, establishes a niche, or introduces this study;
  2. Identify the sentence that states the research gap. If there is none, say so directly;
  3. Check whether the research aim directly answers that gap;
  4. Identify literature review that is unrelated to the gap and could be cut;
  5. List every claim like “no study has” or “for the first time” so I can verify it.
    [Paste your introduction]

When I ran this on the “before” paragraph from the top of this post, the AI said plainly that it contains no sentence stating a research gap.

List problems, don’t rewrite, and you decide what to change.

Prompt 5: Polish, and only polish

Polish the English of the introduction below.
Change the language only. Do not change the argument, the citations, or the strength of the conclusions, and do not add new content.
After editing, list the 3 biggest changes and explain why.
[Paste your introduction]

Say “polish” and nothing more. Don’t add “make it bulletproof for reviewers” or “make it more rigorous”: requests like these push AI into defensive mode, and your conclusions get weaker with every pass.

The last step is yours

After using AI, there is one thing only you can do: open every paper you cite and confirm it actually supports the sentence it is attached to. Elsevier’s AI policy explicitly prohibits using AI to fabricate references, and responsibility for the references lies with the authors, not the tool.

Closing

An introduction is not there to prove how much you have read. It answers three questions: Why does this area matter? What is still unresolved? What did you do?

Dig the gap clearly, and your introduction is most of the way there.

Appendix: my full “find the gap” prompt

Replace the research question, results and papers with your own. The research question and results below are illustrative.

I am writing the introduction of a paper. Below are my research question, main results, and abstracts of the key papers I have read.
Answer using only the material I provide. Do not add other literature:

  1. Summarise what previous work has achieved, citing the paper for each point;
  2. List 3 problems previous work has not solved (research gaps), and say which papers support each gap;
  3. Decide which gap best matches my results, and explain why.
    Do not write the introduction yet.
    [Research question]
    Is college students’ daily short-video use associated with the diversity of information they encounter?
    [Main results]
    A survey of 1,286 college students found that daily short-video use was negatively correlated with information diversity scores (r = -0.32, p < 0.01). Students using short videos more than 3 hours a day scored 27% lower on information diversity than those using them less than 1 hour.
    [Abstracts]
    [1] Zhao & Kou (2024). Effect of short video addiction on the sleep quality of college students: chain intermediary effects of physical activity and procrastination behavior. Frontiers in Psychology, 14, 1287735.
    A survey of 337 college students found that short-video addiction predicted poorer sleep quality, with physical activity and procrastination as partial mediators. Cross-sectional design.
    [2] Boeker & Urman (2022). An empirical investigation of personalization factors on TikTok. Proceedings of the ACM Web Conference 2022, 2298-2309.
    A sock-puppet audit of TikTok’s recommendation algorithm found that language, location, following, liking and watch time all affect recommended content, with following having the strongest effect. The authors discuss implications for filter bubbles but did not measure real users’ information exposure.
    [3] Ribeiro, Ottoni, West, Almeida & Meira (2020). Auditing radicalization pathways on YouTube. Proceedings of FAT* 2020, 131-141.
    An analysis of 330,000+ videos from 349 channels, their comments, and over 2 million recommendations found signs of users migrating from milder to more extreme content; the authors stress the difficulty of causal claims. Data come from public platform data, not user self-reports.
    [4] Fletcher & Nielsen (2018). Automated serendipity: The effect of using search engines on news repertoire diversity and balance. Digital Journalism, 6(8), 976-989.
    Survey data from the UK, US, Germany and Spain show that people who use search engines for news use more, and more balanced, news sources; algorithmic selection does not necessarily create filter bubbles. The study concerns search engines, not short videos.
