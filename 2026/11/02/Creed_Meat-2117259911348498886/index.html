After the GENIUS Act: What Enterprises Still Have to Answer

A close reading of Ray Aria’s 23-interview study: what it can show and what it can’t

After watching a news video of about four minutes, a sales and marketing leader at an environmental construction firm had questions. “Will the FDIC guarantee these? Where are they held at? Who’s holding them?” And then: “what happens if we lose the password.”

The video covered the signing of the US GENIUS Act, the one-for-one dollar backing behind stablecoins, and the chance that regulation could draw in institutions. The same interviewee, P21 in the paper, also asked whether QuickBooks “even has stablecoin” and connected that question to sales tax, audit and Form 1099 reporting.

The exchange comes from Ray Aria’s paper “From authorization to defensibility,” published online in October 2026 in the Journal of Financial Regulation and Compliance. Aria, who teaches at the University of Houston-Downtown, ran 23 short interviews in September and October 2025 to find out how people inside organizations decide whether a newly authorized payment instrument can actually be used.

The scale of the study comes first. This is early qualitative work. It records first reactions to a video from people whose organizations, for the most part, had neither piloted nor acquired stablecoin payment infrastructure. It offers a concept for later research to test and does not measure enterprise adoption. Below I keep the paper’s findings, the strength of its evidence and my own views apart, and I flag where a judgment is mine.

Lawful and defensible are different things

Enterprise payments are controlled activities. They run through treasury procedures and authorization thresholds, post to accounting systems, answer to tax rules, leave audit evidence, pass through sanctions screening and vendor onboarding, and eventually reach the board. Aria starts from a plain observation: a payment instrument can be lawful and technically transferable and still fail the organization’s own test.

The paper calls that test defensibility. Before routine use, an organization must be able to explain why the rail is “lawful, controllable, recoverable and auditable.” The audience for that explanation varies. It may be auditors, boards, legal counsel, banks and regulators, or public payers, employees, donors and patients.

The central concept follows from this: regulatory-compliance readiness lag, which I will shorten to readiness lag. It is the perceived interval between a legal authorization signal and the point at which people in an organization judge a payment rail sufficiently compliant, auditable, interoperable and operationally defensible for enterprise use. Two parts of that definition carry weight. “Perceived” means this is the interviewees’ judgment and not a measured duration. And the endpoint is the ability to explain, evidence and control the rail. Installing a system or making a first payment doesn’t count.

The strongest pattern in the interviews was people granting that regulation made stablecoins more credible while declining to treat that credibility as permission. A digital transformation leader at a large technology firm put it directly: “It does increase the confidence, but does not mean that I will consider to use stablecoins” (P20). Thirteen of the 22 organizational informants drew this kind of distinction. The abstract’s summary reads: “Authorization increased discussability but not defensibility.”

The first gate: is this any use to us?

I think this is the most important move in the paper.

Eight of the 22 organizational informants saw no clear value, or considered stablecoins redundant or a bad deal. A senior advisor in private equity and industrial infrastructure (P03) called them “a solution in search of a problem” and said that converting back into dollars would add brokerage and friction “for no benefit.” A community mental-health chief executive (P08) asked what a one-for-one digital claim would add “since our money already exchanges hands electronically, almost exclusively.”

The paper handles these answers with care. It does not claim that stablecoins lack value. It says legal clarity “cannot compensate for absent relative advantage,” and that where current rails do the job, non-adoption may be “an equilibrium choice rather than a temporary compliance lag.” Readiness lag therefore begins only after an organization identifies a credible use, which the paper calls workflow value. Without one, the process never starts.

Figure 1. The paper’s conceptual structure. The paper did not measure the length of the interval, and line length does not represent time. Based on Aria (2026), Sections 2, 4 and 5 and Table 4.

That boundary matters. Without it, every decision not to adopt could be explained as compliance still catching up, and the concept would explain nothing.

Table 4 contains a detail that is easy to miss. Fifteen people saw plausible value conditional on a use case and eight saw none. That sums to 23, one more than the total, so at least one person falls in both groups. The paper doesn’t pursue this. My reading is that value attaches to a particular payment flow more than to a company: the same person can see no use for stablecoins in payroll and some possibility in a high-fee cross-border corridor. The uses the paper mentions are of that kind: cross-border settlement, high-fee corridors, payment acceleration and working-capital timing.

Keep in mind, too, that “redundant” is the interviewees’ judgment. Aria says plainly that the study did not benchmark stablecoins against ACH, RTP, FedNow, cards, wires or digital wallets on observed cost, speed, reliability or acceptance.

Trust, unbundled

The opening video stressed one-for-one backing and reserve requirements, and the paper is wary of that. Answers that came immediately after the video, or that were explicitly attributed to it, were coded as stimulus-sensitive and establish nothing on their own. What Aria looks for is whether interviewees took the question somewhere else without being led there.

They took it a long way. They asked who issues and holds the asset, how redemption works, who bears liability, which institution stands behind the transaction, how AML/CFT and sanctions duties are met, whether accounting and tax treatment is clear, and what recourse exists when a wallet, custodian, issuer or exchange fails. An international attorney at a global industrial distributor (P18) asked whether US and foreign regimes would be “robust enough” to satisfy anti-money-laundering obligations across jurisdictions. A bank audit-and-review manager (P06) wanted “history” and “testing to failure.” The chief executive of a payroll and HR services provider (P23) was the bluntest: “When you talk about the movement of currency, it has nothing to do with trust. It has more to do with regulation, verification, and sign-offs.”

On this basis the paper rewrites trust as an assurance bundle: reserves, redemption, custody, liability, AML/CFT, sanctions screening, audit evidence, tax treatment, resilience and recourse. For controlled payments, assurance has to be something that can be documented and defended. Eighteen of the 22 mentioned a threshold of this kind.

Figure 2. The ten components come from Section 1 of the paper and the questions from Section 4.3. Based on Aria (2026). Quotations are from the paper.

This finding needs some discounting. Aria concedes that the video probably raised the salience of reserves, and that the weighting reduces this influence without eliminating it. The interview protocol also asked about risks, trust and integration difficulty, so the widening was not entirely spontaneous. Still, I think the bias runs in a helpful direction. The video pulled attention toward reserves, and people moved toward custody, recourse and audit anyway. That counts for more than agreeing with the video would have.

If it can’t be posted, it can’t be used

The third dimension is operational absorbability. A payment has to be posted, reconciled, taxed, audited and supported.

A co-founder of an enterprise software consultancy (P01) anticipated “duplicate parallel processes” if some counterparties used stablecoins while others stayed on ACH or wires. A CFO and cost-reduction consultant (P05) said the challenge went beyond acceptance to “how we record this one, also how we track it,” and how the transaction would connect to accounting and vendor systems. In healthcare the chain runs further, through the electronic health record and the patient-account process (P04).

This dimension does not hold everywhere. Three technology-oriented interviewees (P10, P12 and P17) expected integration to be straightforward once an off-the-shelf platform and training existed. The paper doesn’t treat them as noise. It narrows the finding instead: operational absorbability “is not an inherent property of stablecoins.” It depends on the maturity of connectors, the complexity of existing workflows and the cost of running parallel processes. Sixteen of the 22 mentioned a system or routine constraint.

Waiting on someone else

Interviewees rarely described this as a decision a firm could make alone. The paper calls the parties in the middle gatekeepers and argues that they convert regulation into practical adoption conditions.

Industrial and asset-intensive participants named banks, treasury services, ERP systems, suppliers and anchor customers. A manufacturing CFO (P15) said the company relied on banks for treasury services and could not integrate stablecoins with working-capital functions “until the banks make it available.” Healthcare and nonprofit cases produced a different list. P08 depends on Medicare, Medicaid and insurers, and said their nonparticipation would “preclude” use; the same chief executive questioned whether low-income clients could access such a tool at all. A nonprofit technology executive (P07) was thinking about stewardship of donor funds, fraud protection and donor confidentiality.

Figure 3. Gatekeepers named in the two groups of cases. Both groups are small and do not represent their sectors. Based on Aria (2026), Section 4.5.

The payroll-services case connects the two groups. P23 asked what the clearinghouse would say and described readiness as “alignment across the ecosystem.” The five dimensions traveled across sectors, but their content changed. Heavy industry foregrounded treasury integration and cross-border control. Healthcare and nonprofits foregrounded public-payer eligibility, confidentiality, fiduciary duty and user access.

There is a limit the paper doesn’t stress and that I think is worth stating: these gatekeepers are mostly described by other people. The sample includes one bank audit-and-review manager (P06) and intermediaries such as P23. The paper can’t tell us when banks will offer products or what clearinghouses think. It shows whom people on the demand side are waiting for.

The signal itself is under review

The fifth dimension is signal quality: is the authorization complete, durable, tested and acceptable to the people I answer to? P24, a finance-domain informant used only as context and excluded from the organizational counts, said the legislation raised confidence “not really that much” because it seemed “hastily” enacted and “didn’t address everything.”

A smaller group read the Act as a political signal. A healthcare operations leader questioned whether it reflected consumer protection or political support for crypto. A nonprofit HR executive (P14) expected that “something that Trump passed” might make some organizations “more wary.” The paper says these views were not dominant, but they show how a legal signal can lose weight when boards, donors, patients or employees question its durability or provenance.

That completes the five dimensions. Put together, they give the two lines of notation on the cover. The notation is mine, written from the paper’s definitions; the paper itself contains no mathematical model. V is workflow value, and the interval is undefined when V ≤ 0. The four curves stand for an organization’s judgment on assurance, absorbability, gatekeepers and signal quality, and θ is the threshold it must clear for its accountability audiences, which differs from one organization to another. The paper says the lag closes when these converge. I have written that as the weakest one clearing the threshold, so the heavy line takes the lowest of the four curves. The curves are a sketch, with no scale and no estimated parameters.

How far the evidence reaches

This is an interpretive qualitative study that moves back and forth between interviews and theory to develop a concept. It is neither an experiment nor a sample survey.

The analysis rests on 23 usable interviews: 22 organizational informants and one finance-domain informant. The numbering runs to P24 because P16 yielded no usable transcript. Recruitment ran through professional networks and referrals, and Aria acknowledges the resulting homophily: six of the 22 organizational cases sit in energy- or industrial-adjacent settings, with a regional concentration in Texas. That mix may amplify B2B treasury, cross-border, bank and ERP constraints while underrepresenting consumer-facing, non-Texas and non-US settings. Organization size was not collected consistently.

The interviews were short. Recorded sessions ran 12.9 to 30.7 minutes, with a median of 19.2, video included. Discussion after the video had a median near 15 minutes and covered 12 domains. Split evenly, that is little more than a minute per domain, though time was not split evenly. The paper’s own description is “first-pass, top-of-mind, role-informed judgments,” not deliberated organizational assessments.

One researcher did the analysis, recoding after an interval and reconciling the results, with no second coder. Aria compensates in several ways. Statements tied to real roles and routines got the most weight. Bare repetition of menu options was not used for featured quotations. P19 received extra explanation of potential benefits from the interviewer, so that participant’s later benefit statements were downweighted and not quoted. Every case was classified against each theme as supporting, qualifying, contradicting or not applicable, and negative cases moved the boundaries of themes; that is how workflow value became a separate dimension. Treasury’s advance notice of proposed rulemaking, published on September 19, 2025, fell inside the interview window. No transcript mentions it and individual exposure was not measured, so Aria makes no before-and-after comparison. That was the right call.

Figure 4. Redrawn from Aria (2026), Table 4, as bars, keeping the table’s wording. Counts show evidence density within this corpus and are not population shares. The paper does not publish case-level coding, so the bars show counts only and do not correspond to specific individuals.

Table 4 is the most useful thing to borrow from this paper. Beside each coded pattern sits a column stating what the design does not establish. An 18/22 can’t be read as 82% of American enterprises. It shows only how densely a kind of statement appears among these 22 people.

Four reservations of my own

These are my judgments after reading the paper, not its conclusions.

First, the rise in discussability was not measured. The study has no pre-Act baseline, Table 4 has no such code, and the conversations themselves were started by the researcher with a video and a protocol. Table 4 states that the design does not establish a causal effect of the Act. I would read “increased” in the abstract as interpretation. The supported part is that 13 of 22 separated regulation from readiness.

Second, the lag has no length yet. The protocol covered adoption horizon, but the findings report no specific timing expectations, and they don’t report anyone reaching the end of the interval. The paper says the lag is likely shorter where a bank offers an integrated product and longer where firms operate across borders or depend on public payers. Those remain untested inferences. Aria positions the idea as a sensitizing concept, something that tells researchers where to look and is not yet a validated model. That is more accurate than the tone of the abstract’s “Readiness required.”

Third, the people discussing compliance readiness may not be the people who sign off. The de-identified roles in Table 2 include no treasurer or chief compliance officer. The closest are two CFOs, an attorney and a bank audit-and-review manager; others include a compensation analyst, a sales and marketing leader and an enterprise account manager. Screening prioritized role exposure to payment, finance, legal or compliance work and did not require decision authority. So I read the paper as showing how people around payment workflows imagine compliance. That is related to how a compliance function approves things, but it isn’t the same.

Fourth, two details are worth noting if you cite the paper. The text says six of the 22 cases are energy- or industrial-adjacent. Going by the wording of Table 2, I count at least seven (P02, P03, P10, P11, P15, P17, P18). The categories may simply differ, but the industrial tilt is no lighter than stated. And the published ethics statement still says the approving institution and reference number are “withheld for anonymous review,” wording left over from submission. Neither point affects the main argument.

What happened to the rules after the interviews

This section goes beyond the paper. I added it from primary documents.

When the interviews took place, the Act had been signed but had not taken effect. Section 13 gives regulators one year to issue rules. Section 20 sets the effective date at the earlier of 18 months after enactment or 120 days after the primary federal regulators issue final regulations. When the one-year mark passed on July 18, 2026, the main rules were still proposals.

Figure 5. The vertical axis is drawn to true time scale. Dates come from the paper’s first page, the Federal Register and the OCC.

The paper’s regulatory citations were last accessed on September 3, 2026, and it went online on October 16. Five days later, on October 21, the OCC issued its final rule, the first from a primary federal regulator. It takes effect along with the Act on January 18, 2027.

That rule answers some of what the interviewees asked. Reserve assets, redemption (timely redemption may not exceed two business days), custody, audit and reporting all get specific requirements, though only for OCC-supervised entities. Its limits are just as clear. AML and sanctions are left to a separate rulemaking. When one commenter asked the OCC to address tax treatment, it replied that this fell outside the rule’s scope and the agency’s authority. When commenters asked about digital wallets, money transmission and integration with payment networks, it said that without more detail it could not say those activities were permissible.

Read through the paper’s framework, several items in the assurance bundle are starting to land in rules, while posting, tax reporting and counterparty acceptance were never within the reach of rules like this one. That fits the framework without validating it. The paper itself says later developments concern the same assurance categories but “do not establish that enterprise readiness has followed.” As of November 2, 2026, the FDIC, Federal Reserve, NCUA and FinCEN/OFAC rules I could find in the Federal Register were still proposals, and on the Treasury side there was only an interim final rule on procedures for reviewing state-regime certifications.

Putting it to work

The paper offers a sequential diagnostic: identify the payment pain and compare it with current rails; document the assurance bundle; test ledger, tax, audit, exception and recovery processes; find the gatekeeper whose non-movement blocks use; and assess whether the regulatory signal is complete and durable enough for the organization’s accountability audiences.

Figure 6. Questions and evidence from Aria (2026), Table 1. The order follows Section 6.

Its value, to my mind, lies in classification. Stop at step one and you don’t need the thing, so there is no compliance conversation to have. Stop at step four and your own readiness doesn’t help; the effort belongs with whoever isn’t moving. The paper also suggests that compliance officers and internal auditors treat this as a control-design review, running from authorization and wallet governance through segregation of duties and key management to tax reporting, third-party risk and incident response.

For regulators, the advice is to judge implementation by whether enterprise users can defend payment use as well as by whether issuance is authorized. For banks, custodians and payment providers, it is to package compliance infrastructure with the rail: treasury integration, reconciliation files, transaction reports, screening responsibilities and recovery procedures. These recommendations come from interview analysis and have not been tested in implementation. They work better as a list of questions than as a maturity standard.

The healthcare and nonprofit cases raise a point that is easy to overlook. Modernizing enterprise payments can shift risk onto people outside the organization, such as those without compatible accounts, reliable digital access or confidence in wallet recovery. The paper recommends that early adoption preserve workable legacy channels and not make stablecoin use a condition of access. Faster cross-border transfers and cheaper remittances and donations remain possible, provided interfaces are accessible, consumer protection is clear, conversion is cheap and errors can be resolved.

leaves us with

It doesn’t tell us where enterprises stand today. It has only the first reactions of a group of people in fall 2025. What it leaves is a way of taking a vague question apart: split “why aren’t enterprises using this?” into cases with no use and cases with a use that can’t yet be defended, then break the second group down into assurance, operations, gatekeepers and signal.

A test shouldn’t be far off. Once the Act takes effect in January 2027, if banks begin offering integrated products, the paper’s inference about a shorter lag can be checked against real cases. Aria’s own agenda points the same way: follow organizations from evaluation through pilot, control design and routine use; compare how final rules and integrated bank products affect readiness judgments; and bring in multiple coders.
