Nature Journal: Stop Asking AI to "Polish" Your Paper. Use These 5 Levels and 7 Prompts Instead

发表于2026-11-02|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @DrRabbitAI（Roxy） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

Most researchers use AI for writing with a single prompt: “Polish this paragraph.”

That’s the least useful thing you can ask.

You get back smoother text in seconds. But what did it actually change? Did it shift your meaning? Turn a correlation into a cause? Slip in a claim you never made?

Now you’re the one checking, line by line.

There’s a better way. I teach graduate students and review their drafts every term, and this is the workflow I now give them, with all 7 prompts.

Hi, I’m Roxy. I teach and research AI in higher education, and I build research AI tools. Over a recent holiday I read a commentary in Nature Biomedical Engineering that lays out a much better way to work:

Zhicheng Lin, “Techniques for supercharging academic writing with generative AI,” Nature Biomedical Engineering 9, 426–431 (2025). https://doi.org/10.1038/s41551-024-01185-8

In this piece I’ll do two things: walk you through the paper’s framework, and give you a set of ready-to-use prompts built on it.

A quick note: the framework comes from the paper. The prompts are my own adaptations, based on its ideas and my day-to-day use. Copy them, then tune them for your field and your stage of writing.

01 | Don’t let AI start with the body text

Ask AI to “write a paper on generative AI and higher education” and you’ll get something complete and fluent in seconds.

Read it closely, though, and it’s hollow. No clear research question. Paragraphs that connect on the surface only. Lots of points, little evidence.

A better approach splits writing into three stages you loop through again and again. For each one, here’s what AI can help with, and what only you can judge.

Outlining - AI helps: brainstorming, organizing points, checking structure, offering alternative frameworks - You judge: the research question, the core argument, why the paper matters

Drafting - AI helps: rewriting, summarizing, filling gaps, transitions, alternative phrasings - You judge: whether the content is true, the argument holds, the evidence is enough

Editing - AI helps: grammar, structure, logic checks, mock review - You judge: which changes to accept, and you own the final text

This isn’t a straight line. Find a flaw in the outline while drafting? Go back. Find thin evidence while editing? Go back and add it.

The real lesson isn’t any magic prompt. It’s the workflow: break the paper into small, checkable tasks, and bring AI into each one.

02 | Stage one: Outlining

Use AI two ways here.

To generate: brainstorm what a topic could cover, extend an idea, find different angles on its value.

To evaluate: check whether the outline is complete, whether the parts connect, whether it actually supports your argument.

My take: AI can build the skeleton, but it can’t pick your research question. Give it your question, your argument, your audience and your materials first. Then let it help with structure.

Prompt: Build an outline

I’m writing a paper on [TOPIC].
Research question: [RESEARCH QUESTION]
Core argument: [CORE ARGUMENT]
Target audience / journal: [AUDIENCE OR JOURNAL]
Materials I have: [LITERATURE, DATA, OR CASES]

Do not write any body text yet. Help me draft an outline.

  1. Propose section and subsection headings.
  2. For each section, state what question it must answer.
  3. Explain how the sections connect logically.
  4. Check whether the outline can support the core argument.
  5. Flag gaps in evidence, logical leaps, or points I need to confirm.

Do not add results, references, or data I haven’t provided.

Don’t jump into drafting yet. Switch the AI’s role and have it review the outline:

Prompt: Editor review of the outline

Act as an academic journal editor and review the outline below.

Focus on:

  1. Is the research question clear?
  2. Does every section serve the research question?
  3. Is there repetition, a logical leap, or something important missing?
  4. Will readers clearly see the novelty and value?
  5. Which sections need more literature, data, or cases?

List the problems first, then suggest fixes.
Do not rewrite the whole outline.

Outline: [PASTE OUTLINE]

One principle I use constantly: if you can’t yet judge whether a rewrite is good, ask for suggestions, not rewrites.

03 | Stage two: Drafting

The paper lists the uses: rewriting, summarizing, filling in content, continuing paragraphs, drafting topic sentences, writing transitions, titles and abstracts.

It also suggests asking for several versions at once. You stop accepting the single answer you’re handed, and start comparing, then picking the one closest to what you meant.

My take: this is where AI does the most quiet damage. It doesn’t just fix language. It can add ideas, inflate conclusions, even insert a plausible-looking citation that doesn’t exist.

So write the content yourself first. You supply the research, evidence and argument. AI handles organization and expression.

Prompt: Three versions, no new content

Rewrite the paragraph below in 3 versions:
Version 1: Only necessary grammar and wording fixes.
Version 2: Improve logical clarity and flow.
Version 3: Make it more concise without changing the meaning.

Rules:

  1. Add no new ideas, facts, data, or citations.
  2. Do not change the strength of any claim.
  3. Keep all technical terms, numbers, and citation markers.
  4. After each version, explain what you changed.
  5. If the original meaning is unclear, flag it. Don’t guess.

Original: [PASTE TEXT]

If two paragraphs connect awkwardly, don’t ask for a rewritten chapter. Shrink the task to the transition only:

Prompt: Transition sentences only

Read the two paragraphs below. First explain what logical
relationship should link them, then offer 3 alternative
transition sentences.

Do not edit either paragraph. Do not add new findings.

Paragraph 1: [PASTE]
Paragraph 2: [PASTE]

The key skill here: the smaller and better-bounded the task, the easier the output is to check.

04 | Stage three: Editing the draft

The paper splits editing into two levels.

Basic editing: spelling, grammar, repeated words, sentence flow.

Advanced editing: each paragraph’s point, the order of the argument, whether the introduction targets the research aim, whether the conclusion actually answers the question.

It also suggests having AI play an editor, a professor, or a reviewer to get feedback from different angles.

Most people focus on grammar and word choice. But fancy sentences matter far less than whether the argument holds up.

So before submitting, run a stress test:

Prompt: Mock reviewer

Act as a strict but constructive reviewer for [TARGET JOURNAL].
Evaluate the paper below and answer separately:

  1. Is the research question important?
  2. Where is the novelty compared with existing work?
  3. What evidence supports the core conclusions?
  4. Which conclusions go beyond the evidence?
  5. What are the three issues most likely to get it rejected?
  6. How should each issue be fixed or strengthened?
  7. What must the author verify that AI cannot judge?

Do not invent data, experiments, or references not in the paper.

Paper: [PASTE PAPER OR SECTION]

A mock review is not peer review. It can catch obvious structural problems, but it may miss the debates that really matter in your field. Use it to find problems early, not to predict acceptance.

05 | The five levels of AI writing assistance

The paper also gives a clear five-level framework. Each level hands more to the AI:

  1. Basic editing: spelling, grammar, synonyms. Risk: low
  2. Structural editing: rewriting, translation, flow. Risk: low to moderate
  3. Derivative content: abstracts, titles, analogies, summaries. Risk: moderate
  4. New content: filling in, continuing, expanding, brainstorming. Risk: higher
  5. Evaluation and feedback: finding holes, assessing quality, mock review. Risk: depends on how you use it

Most people are excited about level 4: let the AI write.

From my experience, level 5 is the one researchers should use most.

If you’re worried AI will wreck your paper, have it write less and find problems more.

The paper also separates direct from indirect assistance.

Direct: the AI rewrites your text. Indirect: the AI only suggests, and you make the changes.

You can switch between them anytime. Add one line to any rewriting prompt:

Do not edit or rewrite the original. Only point out problems
and suggest revisions; the author will make the changes.

Your “direct rewrite” instantly becomes lower-risk “indirect assistance.”

06 | Four rules I’d add

Whatever agent you use, whether that’s Codex, Claude Code, Yila or something else, I’d add these four rules.

1. Never write references from the model’s memory.

For any literature review, background section or citation, supply papers you’ve already verified, or let the AI search real papers first. To avoid hallucinated references, I recommend two strategies:

Strategy 1: Connect your agent to a paper-search API. Give Codex or Claude Code access to a scholarly search API, so it looks papers up instead of recalling them from memory.

Strategy 2 (the easier one): Use an agent that’s already connected to paper databases. For example, Yila , which is the tool I use myself. It searches sources like Google Scholar, OpenAlex and Chinese journal databases, which makes it a great fit for researchers.

2. Don’t let AI add academic content silently.

New ideas, data, mechanisms or citations should be clearly marked and confirmed by you. Polishing language must never turn into an invisible rewrite of content.

3. Protect the strength of your claims.

“Associated with” must not become “causes.” “Suggests” must not become “proves.” “May” must not become “will.”

AI sometimes makes sentences punchier by making your paper more certain than your evidence.

4. Keep a change log.

Have the AI output three things together: the revised text, a summary of changes, and risks to review. Never accept smoother text without knowing what it touched.

Here’s a constraint block you can paste at the end of almost any revision prompt:

Preserve all facts, numbers, citations, technical terms,
and the strength of every claim in the original.
Do not add ideas, mechanisms, results, or references
the author did not provide.
Output: (1) the revised version, (2) a summary of the main
changes, (3) risks the author needs to confirm.

Closing

AI still has real problems in academic writing, and hallucinated references are the big one.

But from searching the literature to reviewing it to drafting, it saves a huge amount of repetitive work. The goal isn’t to avoid AI. It’s to learn to steer it: humans judge, AI executes.

Your turn: which stage do you trust AI with least right now, outlining, drafting or editing? And what went wrong the last time you let it?
