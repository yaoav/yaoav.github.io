How I Turn One Image Into a 3D Simulator That Takes Itself Apart

发表于2026-11-06|交易策略
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @Nyxera0x（Nyxera） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

I gave Claude a job description instead of a prompt. Now one picture becomes a 3D build in code that Claude checks against the picture and films taking itself apart. Below: the brief, the checklist and every prompt.

The first time I asked Claude for a robot animal with nothing to work from, I got a generic toy.

This tarantula came out of the same model. What changed is the input: one picture from ChatGPT, and a job description instead of a prompt.

The piranha has 2 rows of glass fangs, a glass flank over its drive gears, a tail stalk that telescopes and an eye under an angry brow. Every part is a button. Press one and that part comes apart.

Every animal here started as one picture from ChatGPT. Claude rebuilt it in three.js. There is no 3D model and no download: every shell, gear and fang is written as code.

Animals are just what I tried first. The same file works on anything with a dense mechanical core: an engine, a drone, a camera, a watch.

I started 9 animals this week. 5 shipped. A moth, a scarab, a fly and a hydra didn’t.

What made the 5 work was a file. I stopped writing prompts and wrote Claude a job description: what the build should look like, where the camera goes, what to check before I see anything, and one rule above all of them. Nothing gets filmed until I say yes.

At the end I’m giving the whole thing away as a free Claude Code skill: the job description, the checklist, every prompt, and 3 of these animals as code you can build and take apart yourself.

What You’re Building

Every build goes through the same 6 pieces:

  • The reference. One image from ChatGPT. It sets the look and nothing else.
  • The job description. One file Claude reads before every build.
  • The checklist. 10 to 12 numbered lines on what this one picture stands on. A new list for every picture.
  • The still. One frame posed like the reference, graded line by line, at least 3 rounds.
  • The page. The object in the middle, a title, one button per part, live cards around it.
  • The film. About 21 seconds: a part comes apart every 2 to 3 seconds, a full explode, an exit. Then a 10-second version for posting, with its own script.

What You Need

  • Claude Code on Opus 5.5.
  • ChatGPT for the reference image.
  • Node.js 20 or newer, plus ffmpeg and Python 3 for the video and the sound. Claude Code installs them if you ask.

Step 1. Generate the Reference

A robotic [hammerhead shark] as a precision machine, studio product shot on a near-white background with a soft floor shadow.
White glossy shells with clearcoat, green only on tips, rims, edges and joint rings.
Dense dark mechanism showing through cut-outs and clear glass: gears, pistons, hoses, small screws.
Eyes as glossy black lenses with a thin green ring. Fins and membranes milky and frosted with thin veins.
Front three-quarter view, the whole subject in frame.

The picture is a target for the look. Claude never imports it: it reads it and builds its own. Swap the subject for anything: [a motorcycle engine], [a camera drone] and [a mechanical watch] run through the same prompt.

Pick a subject with lots of joints and a dense core. Smooth surfaces lose: a long white shell reads as a toy however many rounds it gets.

If it’s a creature, give it one main set of limbs: tentacles, legs or wings. I cut a flying octopus and a scorpion-squid before I made their pictures. Wings and tentacles fighting for the same half second is noise.

Step 2. Write the Job Description

Save this as a file in the project and point Claude at it before every build.

3 lines carry the weight. “Grade against the picture” is how the self-check stays honest. “The film waits for my yes” means no tokens burn on a film of a toy. “The correction goes into this file” is how build 5 stops repeating the mistakes of build 1.

Step 3. Write a Checklist for This Picture

The job description covers every build. The checklist covers one picture. Write it before the first line of code: what does this picture stand on, in numbered lines a reader could check?

My tarantula, 4 of its 12 lines:

Line 5 has the word “never” in it on purpose. Without it the glass turns into one more white shell, because a white shell is the default.

Line 7 is the one I’d write differently. It says thin and never says how thin. The bristles came back as quills, twice the length they should be. The fix is a number: 0.15 to 0.25 of the leg’s thickness. Every fix since has been a sentence with a number in it.

Step 4. Run the Still Loop, Then Grade It Yourself

Then do your own grading, against the picture and not against your memory of it.

That cuts both ways. On the fly I read the eyes as too busy and asked for smaller lenses. The head came back as a disco ball. The reference had big ones; I’d misread it.

Step 5. Measure It, Don’t Eyeball It

A moth, a hydra and a fly all graded themselves dense enough, and all 3 read as clean white diagrams. I threw the moth out after 9 rounds.

The checklist checks features: the eyes, the mouth, the silhouette. It never checks how much machine you can see. So now Claude counts it:

The hydra measured 0.57 of its reference. One round aimed at the number took it to 0.80. The fly reached 0.96 once its plates got real gaps.

Proportions get the same treatment. The piranha’s round 2 passed on density and still came back as a crocodile: jaws at 0.52 of the body length, where the picture’s sit at 0.37. Round 3 got 4 numbers measured off the picture: body height 0.55 of the length, head 0.44, jaws 0.36, a gape as tall as the jaw is long. It landed at 0.35.

Step 6. Save Every Correction

My notes from this batch. Every one went into the job description:

  • The tarantula’s bristles read as quills. Bristles now have a thickness in numbers.
  • A rule to keep the whole octopus in frame pulled the camera back to a flat view from above and curled the arms in. Now only the part being opened has to stay in view.
  • The moth read as a diagram. Density now gets measured, not graded.
  • The piranha read as a crocodile. Proportions now get measured off the picture before the first line of code.
  • A flying octopus and a scorpion-squid had 2 sets of limbs each. One main set per creature now.
  • The camera parked. In the piranha’s first film every teardown sat between 20° and 50° on the same side, at the same height and distance. Every beat now gets its own side, aimed at its own part.

The file grows by one line per mistake. The next build starts with all of them.

Step 7. Film It From One Approved Choreography

My first glass octopus film got another animal’s choreography plus a rule of my own: keep the whole octopus in frame. It came back far away, flat, arms tucked in. A toy.

The second one got an earlier octopus film 1:1 with only the head swapped. That’s the one above. The piranha took an anglerfish’s film the same way: the same deep body, the same jaw that drops off its hinge.

The scarab is the other half of the lesson. Nothing I’d built had a body like it, so it borrowed only a camera and built the body from zero. 4 rounds and about 450,000 tokens later it was still busy and cheap. Now a subject with no close body gets flagged before it starts.

Step 8. Cut What’s Boring, Then Write the Short Version

The whole film is one file:

The hammerhead’s fin came off crooked and the beat was dull, so I deleted 1 line. The film went from 24 seconds to 21.2. The object, the page and the sound all read the same script, so a new cut is one edited file and one render.

For posting I need 10 seconds. My first short versions were the full film trimmed to its busiest moments and crossfaded together. They jumped from fragment to fragment and looked cheap.

Now the short version is its own script: the opening, one part, the explode, the exit.

The tarantula’s scene runs 11.3 seconds and plays back at 1.13x to land on 10. Every short film also gets its own opening and its own exit, so no two in a row look alike: the wasp dives in and climbs out of the top of the frame, the tarantula lands from a jump and jumps at the camera, the octopus jets away.

Every video in this article is one of those: 10 seconds, one take.

Step 9. Render It

A screen recorder drops frames when the GPU works hard. A seeked film can’t. Rendered at 4K and brought down to 1080p, the thin veins on the fins stay sharp.

Bonus. Don’t Run 4 at Once

Every build gets its own fresh agent with the job description, the reference and its checklist. One fresh agent costs less than one long chat, because the long chat carries everything before it into every call.

But more at once isn’t faster. I ran the moth, the hydra, the fly and the octopus in parallel and read each one only after its 6th round. The moth alone ate about 430,000 tokens before I threw it out. A still costs about 400,000 tokens and a film another 150,000 to 400,000, so a late review is the expensive kind.

Now it’s 2 at a time, and I look after round 2, while a note still changes the outcome.

The job description is above. Swap the picture.
