Story Factory: How one mega bento led to an all-new AI studio for marketing

发表于2026-11-04|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @davidhoang（David Hoang） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

How a new approach to the Founder keynote at Team ’26 Europe is helping Atlassian reimagine content creation: what we built in two days, the app it became, and how it works.

Team ’26 Europe is one of Atlassian’s biggest moments of the year, and the Founder keynote is where we share our most important news. Building it usually spans many teams and tools like Figma and Keynote, with a lot of manual curation. This year, two days before the keynote in Amsterdam, we decided to close the show with something we’d never built before.

I was there to lead the Rovo Desktop demo. The new idea: one huge bento, a single scrolling wall summarizing everything we had announced. Hundreds of pieces of work from many teams, moving in concert across three stage screens at a fixed speed, with content changing down to the wire. Thanks to today’s tools and the team’s creativity, we pulled it off.

The team locked the content at 2:51 AM, about six hours before the 9am keynote. The save bar read version 54. That matters because making things today is iterative: we built tools, and a new way of working, that let us move through those iterations faster.

A fully animated bento, customized for the stage, was the deliverable. The tool behind it turned out to be the bigger story. Three weeks later, we’re piloting Story Factory, a way for marketing and design to transform content production in the AI era. We’re building it to be an internal agentic creation studio that turns real front-end code and design tokens into production-grade marketing films, 3D motion, stage visuals, decks and social assets, in minutes, in any language, without screen recordings. We’re not done yet: it’s still in pilot, with more to come.

The real problem was never pixels

The mega bento wasn’t hard because of the design. It was hard because of scale, change and alignment.

  • Scale. Hundreds of pieces of work, each team with its own visual language.
  • Change. Cards were added, swapped and cut until the night before.
  • The venue. Three LED screens across about 65 metres of stage (two 18 × 5 m, one 9 × 5 m, about 10 m apart), one picture 19,440 pixels wide. This was a wall moving across them, so every piece had to work as motion.
  • Alignment. Many people needed to see it, react to it and trust it at the same time.

It wasn’t smooth. The picture was wider than a Mac can display: QuickTime showed those files black, H.264 stopped at 4,096 pixels, and seven seconds of ProRes weighed over 4 GB, so we judged most of it through a cut-down preview.

Designers had dropped everything into one shared Figma file. That was fast, and it was messy. Cleaning it up by hand would have taken more time than we had, so I connected my agent to the file through the Figma MCP. For each card it read the frame’s layers and tokens through the MCP, rebuilt it as a code component, timed its entrance on our motion curves and added it to a shared library, ready to place.

When feedback came in, nobody rebuilt anything. The team iterated on spacing, order and placement to make each card shine on its own and as part of the whole canvas: we dragged a card, saved and played it back. It really came alive when I merged in a Jira board animation my colleague Ee Venn Soh had built. The mega bento stopped being a montage: it morphed from an animation on one screen to a wrap around of all the work shipped across all screens up on stage. That’s the part I’m proudest of: one shared surface where different people’s craft could meet without stepping on each other. A power outage almost nixed it. Instead, it opened the closing keynote, the perfect wrapper to a news-packed event.

Three weeks later: Story Factory

Standing backstage watching the keynote land, we realized a keynote is just a product launch under pressure. The problem we had to solve for the Founder keynote is the same one our marketing colleagues face every week. A launch needs dozens of assets from many teams: a film, a deck, a keynote moment, social cuts, a poster, with reviews from product, creative, PMM and social along the way. They change until the last minute, and they still have to line up with each other and with the product. Same scale, same change, same alignment, only now it never stops.

So we decided to build the tool we made into something every team can use. In its first fifteen days, a handful of us, working with a pilot group of marketers and our agents, landed more than 2,000 commits.

Story Factory turns real product code and Figma designs into reusable, scalable content: films, decks, social posts, print and more, in every market, grounded in brand guidelines and product truth.

You open it and it asks one question: what will you make? A scene, a film, a presentation or stage wall, a social post or a print, or a set of Figma frames to bring in as scenes. Pick one, pick a template and a library to follow, and describe what you want to the Storyteller, the agent inside Story Factory. It writes the story, checks it and keeps every version. Scenes and films share one editor, where length is pace and trim is content.

What makes Story Factory scale

There are good AI video tools, and good design tools with agents in them. Most of them treat a film as something you edit. We made Story Factory scale across Atlassian by training our agent on our product codebases to understand their UI/UX, transitions, motion and features, and we treat a film as something you write: in Story Factory, the film is the code.

01 · Stories are code

Our product UI isn’t screenshots or lookalikes. Agents recreate it from Atlassian’s own front-end code at a pinned version, cite every value, and score the result against real renders. What you preview is what you export, up to 4K, with no screen recording and nothing to drift.

02 · An agent that knows the products

Story Factory starts from real templates and a collection you choose, writes the story, checks it and keeps every version. Ask using descriptive words, fine-tune the result yourself in the editor, and hand your changes back to an agent.

03 · On-brand by construction

The Atlassian Brand library carries the palette, type, logos and motion, and a brand check refuses work that breaks them, down to a logo’s clear space.

04 · One app, every format

A scene is a one-scene film, so everything shares one editor and one timeline. Put a shot in a laptop or a 3D room, present a deck, run a stage wall, export a social cut, or send a film straight to Loom.

05 · Motion that’s on the system

Brand motion reads the Atlassian Design System’s motion tokens, its named curves and timings for how things move. When our Head of Design, Maria Christley, published Atlassian’s motion system, we took its film, measured every word frame by frame and started rebuilding it shot by shot. The finished shots match the original’s curves to within a pixel. Every piece is checked for jumps, jerks and frames out of order before it ships.

How it works

1- Describe a feature, get the launch

Our product marketing team is now ten times more productive. A product marketer describes a feature in a sentence. The Storyteller already knows the product’s codebase, so it draws the real screens, applies the brand guidelines and builds the scene.

From there, the same story becomes whatever the launch needs: a video, a printout, a social post or a presentation, in every market we sell in. People still make the calls: what to make, what good looks like, and when it’s done. The agents do the tedious part.

2- An agent that knows the products

Every library is a set of ready pieces drawn from one product’s real UI or one brand’s real assets. Thirteen collections hold more than 650 of them today. The Storyteller follows the one you pick, so a Confluence film looks like Confluence, down to the pixel.

3- Collaborating and editing

Nothing an agent makes is locked: ask for a change in words, or tune the camera and timing by hand within the brand’s rules, and the agent picks up from there.

4- What it makes

Signature shots, social demos built from the brand team’s guidelines, posters and decks all come from the same pieces, and every word on them is an option you can change.

A poster for the event, a signature take for Loom, and the same poster renamed live: every piece is code, so every word on it can change.

5- One brand, many products, every surface

Creating became easy, but keeping tabs on the brand guidelines immediately became a challenge. Atlassian is one brand with many product brands inside it. Jira, Confluence, Loom, Rovo and the rest each have their own UI, their own words and their own way of moving. Multiply that by dozens of markets and every format marketing needs, from a sales deck to a keynote stage, and you get a really big problem to scale: thousands of assets that have to look like Atlassian and like the product they show, at the same time.

Our take is to build in layers.

  • The Atlassian brand is the foundation. Palette, type, logo rules, the motion system and the brand team’s demo system live in one library, and a brand check holds every story to them.
  • Product brands are infused on top. Each product library carries the product’s real UI and identity, and speaks the master brand’s language for everything around it: the grids, the type, the motion, the end cards.
  • Every surface uses the same pieces. A moment made once becomes a slide in a keynote, a post for X or LinkedIn, an A3 printout, a cut in a launch film and a card on a stage wall across three screens. Placed by reference, it updates everywhere it’s used, unless you choose to keep a version where it is.

6- Every market, every format, themes and more

Because a story is code, it isn’t one video. It’s every version of it, made from the same source.

  • Many markets, many languages. Switch a piece from English to German or Japanese and the product UI changes with it, using the product’s own translations, along with local names, dates and numbers. It’s an easy way to see how a screen holds up in another language before it ships.
  • Every shape. X, LinkedIn and Instagram posts, stories and verticals. 16:9, 1:1, 4:5 and 9:16 cuts. A4 and A3 printouts and posters. Slides, and a stage wall. One export can make every shape at once.
  • Light and dark. Every piece, slide and card has its own appearance, and a card can follow its slide or keep its own. The app’s theme never decides it for you.
  • Options instead of copies. A piece shows what it can change, like its view, its moment, its market or its background. Save a combination you like as a variant, or make it the default.

7- Motion that’s on the system

Motion is where a brand drifts first. Every designer eases a little differently, and after a few hundred assets a company moves in a few hundred ways. We built Story Factory so every asset moves like Atlassian by design.

  • A keyframe is the camera at a moment. How near it is, where it looks, how it’s turned and how high it sits. On a 3D shot, the same controls move the scene’s own camera.
  • Curves from the system. Each move rides a named curve: the brand’s camera curves first, then the brand’s motion, the film’s and the app’s, each drawn as its own glyph.
  • It travels with its shot. Reorder, duplicate or remove a shot, and its camera goes with it.

Built as a team

Story Factory is built in the open, and people from across Atlassian have shipped to it: a teammate’s Jira board animation, browser exports, a dead-code cleanup, whole new libraries. Even our CEO, Mike Cannon-Brookes, has jammed on it with us, and his pull requests went through review like everyone else’s.

We say “Open company, no bullshit” a lot at Atlassian. This is what it looks like in practice: a designer sees a problem, builds the tool, and the whole company is welcome to pitch in.

What we learned

  1. Count your repeats. If you’ve done something three times, build a tool for it.
  2. Name the real problem. Late requests are about scale, change and alignment more than the final frame.
  3. Let AI do the tedious part. Cleanup, measuring and testing can be prompted. Decisions can’t.
  4. Make the product the spec. Draw from the source, and measure against it.
  5. Build for the team. The best tools make room for other people’s craft.
  6. Let it compound. Small tools become building blocks. When the next two-day request lands, most of the work is already done.

Look at what’s around you. Then build for it.
