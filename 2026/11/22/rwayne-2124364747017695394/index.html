Can AI Data Centres Make Australia Great Again?

发表于2026-11-22|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @rwayne（Roland.W） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

When people talk about AI data centres, the first country that comes to mind is almost always the United States. The default assumption is that all the big training clusters live in Virginia, Arizona, or Ohio — and to be fair, the numbers back that up. Second is China, places like Ulanqab in Inner Mongolia, where power is cheap, the weather is cold, and land is wide open for green compute. After that come the Nordics — Norway, Sweden, Finland, Iceland — cold climate by default, plenty of hydro. Then the Middle East, backed by sovereign wealth funds pouring money into AI infrastructure. And in Asia–Pacific, most of the conversation stays fixed on Singapore, Malaysia, Japan, and South Korea.

Almost nobody talks about Australia.

On 23 December 2024, a Blackstone-led consortium paid A$24 billion to buy data centre platform AirTrunk. In February 2026, Blackstone came back again, leading a US$10 billion private debt facility for Australian AI factory builder Firmus. Two months later, in April 2026, McKinsey published Australia’s AI moment: Building Asia–Pacific’s compute hub. Their estimate: Australia’s data centre demand is set to climb from 1.5 GW in 2025 to 5.0 GW by 2030. Getting there takes up to A$190 billion in new capital investment, which could add roughly A$80 billion a year to GDP and support around 100,000 new jobs.

Over the past three decades, Australia’s GDP per capita grew at an average of 1.7% a year. After 2016 that dropped to 0.6%. Since 2020, it has even dipped negative. McKinsey calculates that digital infrastructure investment alone could close nearly 40% of the private capital gap Australia needs to get back to long-run productivity growth. Around the same time, NVIDIA announced partnerships with eight Australian operators to build up to 2 GW of local AI infrastructure by 2027. Across the country, data centres sitting in planning applications now total 8,135 MW — 15.7 times Australia’s publicly disclosed live capacity.

My view here is straightforward: I am bullish on Australian AI data centres.

Three concrete facts sit behind that call. First, the under-construction pipeline and long-term contracted order books prove the demand is real. Second, high grid barriers, daytime surplus green power, and Asia–Pacific compliance rules give Australia a rare locational edge. Third, institutional capital has cleanly separated “owning the building” from “buying the chips”, so the infrastructure asset outlives the GPU depreciation cycle.

Under-Construction Capacity Is 1.7x Live Stock, and Liquid Cooling Has Barely Started

A data centre is a specialised industrial building housing servers, power distribution, and cooling systems. The industry measures size by the electrical power delivered strictly to servers and compute chips — IT load — measured in megawatts (MW), with 1 gigawatt (GW) equal to 1,000 MW. Add cooling and power distribution overhead on top, divide total facility power by IT load, and you get Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE). Closer to 1 means less wasted power; Australian industry estimates typically use 1.3. To put that in everyday terms, 1 MW of continuous load is roughly what several hundred ordinary households pull when appliances are running. A 100 MW campus draws about as much power as a medium-sized regional town.

Media coverage loves lumping live facilities together with projects that just lodged a planning application yesterday. When I went through the public Australian records up to 18 November 2026, removed duplicates, and cut out tiny edge sites, there were 159 standalone data centres above 1 MW nationwide. Split by actual engineering stage — live, under construction, approved, in planning, and announced.

Even on live stock alone, three widely cited numbers sit far apart: my bottom-up public count is 519.3 MW, the Australian Energy Market Operator (AEMO) implies roughly 439 MW from actual metered electricity use, and commercial tracker DC Byte puts total live capacity at 1.6 GW.

Where the real expansion shows up is under construction and approved. Right now, publicly disclosed capacity under construction sits at 892.3 MW — 1.7 times the 519.3 MW of disclosed live stock. Another 1,583 MW already holds formal planning approval, including NEXTDC’s Sydney S4 (250 MW under construction in a single phase) and CDC’s Marsden Park campus (504 MW approved). These projects have either broken ground or cleared the gate to start building.

AEMO’s metered data shows average monthly data centre electricity demand across the NEM climbed from under 200 MW in 2018 to more than 700 MW in 2026 — over 3.5 times higher in eight years. ASX-listed leader NEXTDC’s FY26 results give an even cleaner proof point: 288 MW built across the group (278 MW in Australia), 175.0 MW currently billing, and a contracted forward order book sitting at 565.1 MW. That forward order book is 3.2 times current billing capacity and almost double its entire built footprint. Real long-term contracts are backing this build-out.

This also explains why installed capacity always looks bigger than actual power draw on any given day. When a hyperscaler moves into a campus, it wants guaranteed room to expand on the same site over the next five years. So it signs a 5- to 10-year phased ramp contract up front, locking in the substation’s power allocation from day one. Existing campuses have already sold out their substation limits on paper; any new customer demand forces operators to keep building fresh sites.

Over the past two or three years, Australia’s first wave of AI compute mostly went into traditional colocation halls — local GPU cloud operator Sharon AI, for example, simply rented space inside NEXTDC’s Melbourne M3 facility to run its clusters. Out of 159 facilities nationwide, 148 are traditional multi-tenant colocation centres. Only 9 are purpose-built standalone AI facilities. Tasmania’s Firmus Launceston campus (84 MW) is the first one actively under construction; the other 8 are still working through planning and approvals.

McKinsey notes that traditional enterprise server racks draw 4 to 9 kW, while new-generation AI racks jump to 40 to 300 kW. Take NVIDIA’s GB300 NVL72 rack: 72 GPUs and 36 CPUs packed into a single cabinet, pulling 155 kW at peak and roughly 135 kW rated. Concentrating the heat output of dozens of commercial air conditioners into one rack footprint leaves legacy air-cooled halls unable to cope. Direct-to-chip cold plates or immersion liquid cooling become mandatory. As for the water worry people keep bringing up, McKinsey’s report points out that even at 5.0 GW, data centres would still account for a tiny fraction of Australia’s total water use. Closed-loop liquid and immersion cooling cut water consumption sharply and can run on non-potable water. At this scale, national water supply can handle it.

High Grid Barriers Keep Out Destructive Competition, While Green Power and Compliance Pull Regional Spillover

Buying industrial land for a large data centre in Australia is fairly straightforward. What actually holds everything up is getting a high-voltage connection onto the main grid.

Across all 159 facilities, only 3 explicitly state in public filings that their power is already locked in. Almost everyone else is sitting in a grid connection application or queue. AEMO’s numbers show roughly 225 projects in development across the NEM, representing 67 GW of connection enquiries, with a 36% historical cancellation rate. McKinsey’s data lines up with that: getting high-voltage power connected in core metros takes 2 to 3 years on average, construction permits take around 400 days, and 9 of Australia’s 24 major transmission projects are delayed by nearly 2 years on average. Plenty of big schemes have stalled or been scrapped lately — the ACT rejected environmental approval for CDC’s 576 MW Hume campus, AirTrunk withdrew its 140 MW Mars Road proposal in Sydney’s Lane Cove West, and a 1,200 MW campus in South Australia paused its approval process.

When a project is capital-heavy and uncertain, the rational move is to pay a small upfront fee to hold the option to build later, instead of sinking huge construction capital immediately. Economists Avinash Dixit and Robert Pindyck call this the option to wait in Investment under Uncertainty. Australia’s 8,135 MW of planning applications — 15.7 times live stock — has a lot of those early waiting options baked in. Watching a third of them wash out in planning and grid queues is completely normal.

Flip that around. If any developer could pull high-voltage power in three months and throw up a hall, data centres would have slid into a price war and oversupply years ago. Whenever a critical input has inelastic supply, excess returns from downstream growth flow straight to whoever controls that scarce input. Multi-year substation upgrades, strict environmental reviews, and mandatory renewable-plus-storage rules clear out over a third of applicants before a single slab is poured. The campuses that already hold permits and live high-voltage connections sit on real scarcity and steady pricing power.

High barriers are what keep out destructive competition. That holds for big businesses and small ones alike.

Australia’s power mix adds an unexpected twist. Outsiders often assume Australia is short of electricity. In reality, daytime wholesale power across southern Australia is oversupplied. Because rooftop solar penetration is massive — stacked on top of dense wind and utility-scale solar farms — wholesale spot prices drop to zero or negative over and over again. AEMO’s Quarterly Energy Dynamics shows that in Q4 2025, zero or negative prices hit 48.4% of dispatch intervals in South Australia and 43.1% in Victoria, with the NEM-wide average at 31.0%. By Q1 2026, during daytime hours (9 am to 5 pm), negative prices hit 67% of intervals in South Australia and 53% in Victoria.

Wait — two-thirds of the daytime, green power on the wholesale market is so oversupplied that the price goes negative????

Federal Climate Change and Energy Minister Chris Bowen cited official forecasts noting data centres currently use around 2% of Australia’s grid electricity, projected to reach 6% by 2030 — roughly equal to the annual power consumption of every household in Victoria. That is why the federal government is pushing a “bring your own new renewables and storage” framework, requiring large facilities to fund their own new green generation and batteries so households do not carry the upgrade bill. For data centres paired with big batteries, that is a direct tailwind. Wind and solar generators have struggled for years with daytime curtailment and a shortage of creditworthy buyers willing to sign 10- to 15-year power purchase agreements. Data centres are the exact anchor load they have been waiting for. You can already see the shift in AEMO’s Q3 2026 report: negative price intervals dropped back to 22.0% in South Australia and 17.8% in Victoria, driven by higher daytime load and grid-scale batteries soaking up surplus solar. Quinbrook’s Supernode project in Queensland — 500 MW of data centre capacity paired directly with a 780 MW battery and locked-in power — is built straight on top of that energy logic.

Power and land are also redrawing Australia’s internal compute map.

Right now, 96% of existing data centre electricity demand sits in two eastern metros: NSW (including the ACT) takes 66%, and Victoria takes 30%. Sydney and Melbourne have mild climates and sit away from major earthquake zones. Sydney is the main international subsea cable landing hub for the southern hemisphere, handling day-to-day inference for ANZ banking, government, and enterprise workloads. On top of that, the Australian government has put over A$500 million into Pacific subsea cables, extending sovereign cloud and edge reach across the South Pacific.

Large-model training, which does not care much about a few tens of milliseconds of latency, is heading straight to where cheap energy and land live. Tasmania — under 1% of existing data centre load — used its cool climate and hydro power to land Australia’s only standalone AI factory currently under construction (Firmus Launceston, 84 MW). South Australia — only 2% of existing load — sits on abundant wind and solar and now leads the nation with 3,500 MW of announced capacity. The Northern Territory runs its own separate grid and is tapping local gas fields to push campuses above 1,000 MW with dedicated on-site power plants. Building a data hall is fast; stringing high-voltage interstate transmission lines is painfully slow. Putting training factories right next to power stations and gas fields — moving data over fibre instead of pushing electricity across congested grids — bypasses the eastern seaboard bottleneck altogether.

Zoom out to Asia–Pacific, and the external demand flowing toward Australia gets even clearer.

McKinsey projects that by 2030, Europe’s five core hubs (Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris, Dublin) will face a 3.0 GW supply-demand gap, pushing around 60% of new AI demand north to the Nordics. In Asia–Pacific, Singapore — constrained by land, power quotas, and strict energy rules — already hit a 0.8% vacancy rate in 2024 and faces a roughly 2.0 GW gap by 2030. Spillover went to Southeast Asia first: Malaysia pulled in over US$20 billion of North American hyperscaler investment in the first ten months of 2024, driving Johor’s vacancy down to 0.7% while regulators began adding permit checks on US AI chip transshipment. Thailand approved roughly US$10 billion in data centre applications in the first half of 2025 as well.

Then geopolitical compliance tightened — and handed Australia a massive opening. Through 2026, Washington kept closing loopholes on advanced chip exports and remote cloud access: the US Bureau of Industry and Security enforced strict global controls on restricted-group entities buying B300 and Rubin chips, the US House passed the Remote Access Security Act, and in July 2026 NVIDIA audited Asian facilities and buyers in person, cutting more than half of its existing Asian buyer list. As a Five Eyes ally, Australia sits on the US chip export whitelist — importing B300-class hardware by destination requires no US licence. Pair that with Australia’s Defence Trade Controls Act and federal Hosting Certification Framework, and the legal boundaries are crystal clear. As Southeast Asian hubs face tighter transshipment checks and power limits, Australia stands out as one of the very few places in Asia–Pacific where Western hyperscalers can reliably land top-tier chips and sign 15-year leases without losing sleep over compliance.

Even the cost picture — the part people most often get wrong — looks very different once you pick the right benchmark. Compared against the Asia–Pacific average, McKinsey points out that Australian commercial power is 56% dearer and construction labour is 159% higher, making long-run build-and-operate marginal costs roughly 15% above low-cost markets like Malaysia or India. Compare Sydney against tier-one developed hubs like Singapore and Tokyo, though, and the table flips completely. CBRE’s Global Data Center Trends 2026 puts wholesale colocation asking rents at US$403/kW/month in Singapore, US$280/kW/month in Tokyo, and US$188/kW/month in Sydney.

Sydney’s wholesale colocation rent is less than half of Singapore’s and about a third below Tokyo’s. AI chips are priced rigidly in US dollars everywhere on earth, while Australian land, civil works, local power, and engineering wages are all paid in Australian dollars. At the RBA exchange rate on 18 November 2026 (A$1 = US$0.6720), for US-dollar capital, Sydney is a rare value play among developed hubs.

Infrastructure Outlives the Chip Cycle

Most people’s biggest worry about AI data centres boils down to one line: AI chips depreciate and turn over every three or four years — if billions go in today, won’t it all get written down in a few years?

Fixed capital in a data centre splits cleanly into two buckets: the building shell plus mechanical and electrical (M&E) fit-out, and the chip servers sitting inside the racks. Combining Turner & Townsend’s 2026 Sydney construction cost index, the RBA exchange rate, and market pricing for NVIDIA GB300 NVL72 racks (US$3.7 million to US$6.5 million per rack).

Building a 10 MW liquid-cooled hall in Sydney runs an estimated A$145.4 million to A$202.2 million — only 7% to 10% more than an air-cooled hall of the same size (A$135.8 million to A$183.8 million). Spending less than 10% extra on M&E lets the building host A$350 million to A$650 million worth of GB300 hardware. Out of Australia’s 159 facilities, only 8 publicly confirm liquid cooling and 4 are hybrid air-liquid. New campuses have opened a real technical gap over older air-cooled buildings.

Inside that same 10 MW liquid-cooled hall, buying the chips costs 1.74 to 4.46 times as much as building the entire facility. McKinsey’s A$190 billion national investment breakdown shows the exact same split: IT hardware takes 64% (A$122 billion), while M&E systems (A$30 billion, 16%) and civil construction (A$17 billion, 9%) together make up 25% (A$47 billion) — hardware is 2.6 times the shell and M&E. More importantly, their lifespans sit in different worlds. A building with a live high-voltage substation and liquid-cooling loops runs for 20 to 30 years and can host whoever rents it next. The silicon inside the racks faces next-generation obsolescence in 3 to 4 years.

Because of that split, the Australian market naturally sorted itself into clear lanes. Across 68 active players nationwide, 32 are traditional colocation operators and 21 are energy or property developers — 53 companies combined, almost 80% of the field. Emerging GPU-cloud neoclouds number just 4. Every single one of the 18 providers on the federal government’s Certified Strategic hosting list is a traditional colocation, hyperscale cloud, or telco incumbent. Emerging AI compute players almost universally rent halls from those incumbents — Sharon AI, for instance, raised a reported US$356 million GPU-backed debt facility to buy hardware and deployed inside NEXTDC’s Melbourne M3 rather than building its own substation from scratch.

Blackstone’s two Australian deals spell out the whole playbook better than any textbook.

On 23 December 2024, Blackstone’s consortium paid A$24 billion in equity to buy AirTrunk outright across Asia–Pacific — owning 20-plus-year power-connected buildings to collect long-term rent growth and asset appreciation. Then in February 2026, when Firmus needed capital to buy 3- to 4-year-cycle chips for its up-to-1.6 GW Australian AI factory rollout, Blackstone switched instruments: leading a US$10 billion private debt package alongside Coatue, taking fixed interest and leaving chip price risk on the downstream customers and equity holders.

Once concrete and high-voltage gear are poured on a specific site, repurposing them is brutally expensive, which normally leaves asset owners exposed to hold-up by tenants. A substation and liquid-cooled hall in Western Sydney actually have higher site specificity than servers you can unbolt and truck away. That is exactly why mature Australian operators refuse to build on speculation: before pouring concrete, they lock in 10-year-plus pre-commitments with hyperscalers, spend the extra 7% to 10% on liquid-cooling readiness so the building stays chip-agnostic, and push short-lived hardware depreciation cleanly onto the tenant’s balance sheet.

At CBRE’s Sydney benchmark of US$188/kW/month, a 10 MW facility generates roughly A$33.57 million in annualised wholesale colocation rent — about a quarter of the lower-bound air-cooled build cost (A$135.8 million). Steady, inflation-linked cash flows over decades: classic core utility infrastructure.

Australia’s longest-horizon domestic capital is already deep in this sector. Local champion CDC Data Centres was valued at A$13.7 billion on a 100% basis in its early-2025 equity transaction. On its share register, Australia’s sovereign wealth fund the Future Fund holds 34.53%, and federal public-sector super fund CSC holds 12.04% — two national long-term institutions holding 46.57% together. The federal National Reconstruction Fund Corporation committed up to A$200 million to Macquarie Technology Group; NEXTDC raised A$9.75 billion of new capital across FY26; DigiCo bought Global Switch’s Australian assets for A$2.12 billion.

For Australia’s massive domestic superannuation pool, this is close to an ideal asset class: 15- to 20-year duration, denominated in Australian dollars, with zero currency-hedging drag. Deep local long-term capital, abundant green power and land, and Five Eyes chip access sitting inside one developed country — you can count on one hand how many places on earth have that combination.

Right now, new campuses in Western Sydney and Tasmania are already going up. Blackstone’s A$24 billion and nearly half the equity of a national champion backed by Australia’s sovereign fund are already in the ground.

And there is plenty more coming.

The direction of this upcycle is already set — not much left to debate. From here, it just comes down to how fast the high-voltage transmission lines and substations actually get built.
