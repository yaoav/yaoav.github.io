Open-sourcing lemo-animi, a stop-motion skill: one sentence, and Codex makes the film

发表于2026-11-19|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @lemomo_ai（Lemomo） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

“Make a 40-second claymation: a little penguin learns to make pancakes.”

That’s the whole prompt. The agent writes the story and the shot list, uses an image model to make the characters, sets and action sheets, then brings them to life frame by frame in code. It scores the film with real instrument samples, adds foley, and finally opens a local review desk where you give notes until the film is done.

This is lemo-animi, the open-source project I released today: a stop-motion plugin that works in both Codex and Claude Code.

Below are eight materials and eight sample films. Each one was made by the skill itself, starting from a single sentence.

1. What lemo-animi is

One sentence in, one stop-motion short out. It’s an agent skill for Codex and Claude Code. The main way to use it is making films in Codex, with Codex’s built-in image generation. Claude Code works too, paired with an image model.

  • The image model draws the character sheets, 8-frame action sheets and backgrounds.
  • Python slices the action sheets into single frames, times them on twos (or threes) over the set, and adds camera moves, contact shadows, lighting and grading. This part is the heart of stop-motion.
  • Each material is a fixed style package: prompt templates, lighting, camera language, score and foley.
  • An original score written with real instrument samples, foley on every contact, and narration and subtitles if you want them.
  • Not happy with the cut? Stop the review desk at the exact moment and write your note there. It fixes every note and shows you a new version, until you click Approve.

Stop-motion is shot one frame at a time. That little stutter is the whole charm.

Models: in Codex, GPT-6.2 Sol or GPT-6.2 Astra at High reasoning is recommended; GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra (High or above) also work.

Narration (an online voice) and the review desk (a local port) need a Codex mode that allows network access and permission requests. If permissions fall short, it stops before it starts and tells you what to allow.

Claude writes and runs the code, but it doesn’t draw. By default the images come from Codex on your machine. You can also configure another image model, or a local one.

Your first film

The first film needs the network. If nothing is installed yet, it downloads about 1.7 GB: a Python runtime (about 300 MB) and the instrument samples for the score (about 1.4 GB, resumable), all in ~/lemo-animi-env.

This step is automatic: the agent sets up and checks the environment before it asks you anything. If a download fails, it tells you why and stops to wait.

Once you’ve answered its questions, it waits for the install to finish and pass its check, then starts the film. Nothing already installed is downloaded again. Codex and Claude Code on the same machine share this environment, so whichever installs it first saves the other the download.

3. 8 styles, 8 sample films

Each sample was made by the skill itself from one sentence, with Chinese narration and subtitles. You can name a style by its Chinese name, English name or id.

01 · Clay (clay): “Bobo’s Pancake”

Characters and sets sculpted from oil-based clay. Big expressions, squash and stretch; the funniest of the eight.

Story (53 s): Bobo the little penguin wants to flip the perfect pancake. Two tries, two flops; eyes shut, the third one lands. Then the pancake stuck to the ceiling drops down, right to a little mouse.

02 · Papercraft (papercraft): “The Swimming Lighthouse”

A paper-cut lightbox of cut, scored and folded card, layered front to back and glowing from behind. Quiet and dreamy.

Story (57 s): A little fox heads out to sea with a paper lantern. A wave knocks the lantern into the water; in the pitch dark, a big whale nudges it back up. Turns out the whale is afraid of the dark too.

03 · Tin toy (tin): “Dudu’s Wind-Up”

Retro wind-up toys of printed, stamped tin that move in little jerks. Wind them up and they come alive; when the spring runs down, they stop.

Story (55 s): After closing time at a tin circus, a little elephant balances a ball. On its highest toss the spring runs down; it judders to a stop, and the ball lands right on the tip of its tilted trunk.

04 · Felt (felt): “A Little Lamp on a Snowy Night”

Needle-felted little animals and snowscapes. Fuzzy, quiet and warm: one warm lamp in a cold blue night.

Story (57 s): A little rabbit leaves its lamp with a squirrel who’s afraid of the dark, and walks home in the dark. Just when it can’t go any further, a light comes on behind it.

05 · Cardboard (cardboard): “Cardboard Moon Landing”

A kid’s imaginary world built from shipping boxes, tape and markers. A bit wonky, played completely straight.

Story (58 s): A cardboard astronaut lands on the moon, plants the flag and salutes. The camera pulls back: it’s all on the bedroom floor. “Actual distance flown: 30 centimeters.”

06 · Wooden puppet (wood): “Under the Lamp”

Varnished, carved, jointed wooden puppets coming to life one joint at a time under an oil lamp late at night.

Story (55 s): A freshly carved little wooden bear wakes under the oil lamp, learns to stand, and gets scared back by the dark. As the wick burns low, it turns the lamp up, and in the light a little wooden rabbit wakes too.

07 · Crochet (crochet): “Pupu Puffs Up”

Dolls and a whole little world crocheted stitch by stitch. Candy colors, busy, everything gently moving.

Story (58 s): Whenever the little pufferfish gets excited, it balloons into a spiky ball and scares all the small fish away. When a big current sweeps in, it puffs up on purpose, and all the little fish hide behind it.

08 · Fabric patchwork (fabric): “Doudou’s Shortcut”

Rag dolls and countryside made of printed cotton, buttons and stitches. A walk in warm sun, soft and homespun.

Story (58 s): The little duck at the back of the line gets lost. It follows a line of stitching across the patchwork fields and crosses the river on buttons. This time, it’s the first one to reach the pond.

4. How it works: from one sentence to a film

  1. Set up the environment, then every question at once (style, length, aspect ratio, narration, title and end cards), with the defaults listed first; or just reply “all defaults”. From there it works straight through to the finished film, with only brief progress notes.
  2. Plan first. Story skeleton with seconds per part (setup, three tries, low point, turn), beats, shot list, image list.
  3. Generate images. Character sheets, backgrounds, 8-frame action sheets and 2×2 close-up sheets, on transparent backgrounds, all in the same material.
  4. Code makes it move. Action sheets sliced into single frames and timed on twos (or threes), plus camera moves, contact shadows, lighting and grading.
  5. Sound. An original score from real instrument samples, foley on every contact; narration and subtitles if you want them.
  6. You review. It reviews and fixes the film once on its own first, then opens the review desk for you.

The longer the film, the more shots and images:

  • 20 s: 2–4 shots, about 10–14 images
  • 40 s (default): 5–8 shots, about 20–26 images
  • 60 s: 6–12 shots, about 30–38 images
  • 2 min: 12–22 shots, about 55–68 images

If you want a richer, more complex plot, ask for it up front when you start.

It talks to you in whatever language you write in; narration, subtitles and title cards are in Chinese or English.

Films default to 1920×1080 (portrait 1080×1920, square 1080×1080) at 24 fps, with an original score and foley, saved in a film folder the agent creates in your working directory.

5. Using the review desk

The review desk: the film on top, a filmstrip laid out by shot underneath, notes on the right.

When the film is ready, the agent opens the review desk in your browser. It takes four steps:

  1. Stop at the moment you want changed. Space plays and pauses; ← → step one frame at a time.
  2. Press Enter and write what you see. Timecode, frame number and shot are filled in automatically, and a pin drops on the filmstrip.
  3. Click “Send to agent”. The agent works through each note, replies under it with what it changed, and registers a new version; the page tells you to refresh. Earlier versions and their notes are kept below.
  4. Happy with it? Click “Approve”, and the agent delivers the film.

No rush. The top of the page shows whether the agent is waiting for you and how much time is left; it waits up to 30 minutes after opening the review desk. Going past that is fine, nothing gets lost:

  • Notes are saved to the film folder as you write, and the page keeps working.
  • When you’re done, click “Send to agent” or “Approve”, then tell the agent in the conversation that you’ve finished watching. It reads all your notes and carries on.
  • If you closed the page, ask the agent to reopen the review desk.
  • If the review desk opened as a local file (the agent’s sandbox wouldn’t let it start a local server), click “Copy notes” and paste them into the conversation.
