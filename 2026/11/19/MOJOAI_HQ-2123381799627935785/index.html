This is how you should set up your Anthropic IPO trading plan (prompt included)

发表于2026-11-19|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @MOJOAI_HQ（MOJO） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

The night before Anthropic’s first trade on Nasdaq, one share of the company was already trading at $104.8 on Hyperliquid. The IPO price was $83. That’s a 26% premium on a stock that didn’t exist yet.

We asked MOJO about that number in a single chat. Our first question was a bad one: “What should I do tonight?” It took eight messages to turn it into something an agent could run. This post walks through those eight messages, and the six habits behind them. The trade is the example. The conversation is the point.

Key takeaways

**1. Ask for scenarios, not a call. **”Should I buy?” gets an opinion. “What happens at each price?” gets a plan.
**2. Put your risk in dollars. **”$50 per trade” turns every scenario into an exact position size.
3. When something looks backwards, ask why. The answer often changes the plan.
**4. Say how you like to trade. “**In and out fast” changes stops, targets and exits.

One-shot prompt is at the end of article. 🫡

The setup: a stock that trades before it lists

A pre-IPO perpetual (or perp) is a futures-style contract on a company that hasn’t listed yet. On trade.xyz, one of the builder markets on Hyperliquid, the contract xyz:ANT tracks one Anthropic share. Before listing, its price comes only from people trading it. Once the real stock trades, the contract switches to follow the Nasdaq price.

So the perp price is a bet on where the first real trade will land. Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 on 18 November, above its $70–78 range. At 02:23 PT / 10:23 London / 18:23 SGT / 19:23 KST on 19 November, the perp sat at $104.8. In other words, the market was pricing a first-day pop of about 26%.

Nasdaq opened at 06:30 PT / 14:30 London / 22:30 SGT / 23:30 KST. Big IPOs usually print their first trade hours later, around midday New York time. That gap, between the open and the first print, was the window we were planning for.

1: ask for scenarios, not a call

The first message asked for a strategy. The useful part of the answer wasn’t a direction. It was a map: three things that could happen at the first print, and who gets hurt in each.

  • The stock opens above $110. The perp has to rise, and short sellers get squeezed.
  • It opens between $95 and $110. The perp was roughly right, and the main risk is the jump when the price source switches.
  • It opens below $95. The perp has to fall 10% or more, and leveraged buyers get liquidated.

It also gave context. The largest IPOs have opened anywhere from flat (Facebook, 2012) to up about 38% (Alibaba, 2014). Pricing above the range is a strong-demand signal. But a 26% premium already counts most of that good news.

The lesson is in how the question changed. “What should I do?” asks the other side to make your decision. “What could happen, and what does each outcome do to me?” asks for something you can check. An agent is much better at the second.

2: zoom in until the numbers are useful

Three scenarios are a start. The second message asked for more: “Give me more up and down cases.”

The answer was a ladder of nine first-print prices, from a broken IPO at $74.7 to a doubling at $166. Each row showed how far the perp would move from $104.8, what that did to a 5x long or short, a rough probability, and what kind of day would produce it.

Two things fell out of that grid that the three-scenario version hid. The middle outcome sat slightly below the current price, but the upside tail was longer than the downside. So a plain short was cheaper to get wrong than it looked. And the answer corrected an earlier number: at 10x leverage, a move of about 5% against you liquidates the position, not 10%. Finer questions catch coarser mistakes.

Leverage means trading a position bigger than your money. At 5x, $200 controls $1,000 of contract. Liquidation is the exchange closing your position because your margin can no longer cover the loss.

3: put your risk in dollars

The third message asked how much to put into each scenario out of $1,000. That’s a personal decision. The useful reply was a method instead of a number:

Position size = the most you’ll lose on this trade ÷ the distance to your stop

Leverage only decides how much margin you post. The amount you lose is set by your risk and your stop. And the liquidation price must always sit beyond the stop, or the exchange closes you before your stop does.

The next message gave the missing input: “$50 of risk per trade.” That one number turned every row of the ladder into exact orders. A 4.5% stop means $50 ÷ 4.5% = $1,111 of contract. At 3x that’s $370 of margin. At 5x it’s $222.

This is the most useful sentence you can give an agent. “Be careful” means nothing to it. “Never lose more than $50 on a trade” can be checked, every time.

4: say how you like to trade

The seventh message changed the plan’s character: “I just want a quick taste. In and out fast.”

The first draft aimed to ride the perp all the way to the Nasdaq price. The quick version takes only the first part of the move:

  • One target instead of two. Close 100% at −3%, not half at −9% and half at the indicated price.

  • A tighter stop. +2.5% instead of +4.5%. Same $50 risk, so the position grows to $2,000.
  • A time stop. If the target isn’t hit within 10 minutes, close at market. A gap trade lives on timing. If the gap hasn’t closed in 10 minutes, someone else took it, and holding on turns it into a bet on direction.
  • A breakeven rule. Halfway to the target, if the price stalls, move the stop to the entry price.
  • An emergency exit. If the indication is revised above $100, the reason for the trade is gone. Close immediately.

The eighth message went further: “Make all of them quick in and out.” Every setup was rebuilt the same way, and the default leverage moved from 3x to 5x. With $2,000–2,500 positions, 3x would have needed up to $833 of margin out of $1,000. At 5x it’s at most $500, and liquidation sits about 14% away, well beyond any stop.
