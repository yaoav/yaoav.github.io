AutoHoG: the FHE engine for confidential onchain finance

发表于2026-08-27|行业观察
|浏览量:

本文转载自 X 博主 @primus_labs（Primus Labs） 的长文，原文链接，版权归原作者所有。

A confidential transfer on fully encrypted 64-bit balances now completes in about 90 milliseconds on our stack. Eight NVIDIA RTX 5090s, consumer GPUs you can buy off the shelf, sustain more than 210 of them per second, and every card you add is pure additional throughput. For years, numbers like these were the missing piece between “FHE is the future of privacy” and FHE actually powering confidential onchain finance.

This post is the story of how we got there: a scheme, a compiler, and a GPU engine, designed and built in-house at Primus.

The promise, and the wall

Fully Homomorphic Encryption lets a network compute directly on encrypted data. Balances, amounts, and logic stay encrypted at every step. Nobody sees anything, yet everything still works. It is the cleanest answer to on-chain privacy that cryptography has ever produced.

It was also slow, and the reason lives in one operation. In gate-based FHE, encrypted logic runs as a boolean circuit, and every gate requires a bootstrapping: a refresh procedure that keeps ciphertext noise under control. Two distinct types of bootstrapping emerged early: one introduced by FHEW [1], and a different construction known as GINX [2], co-authored by one of our own team. TFHE [3] then took the GINX approach and refined it, bringing two breakthroughs at once. It made bootstrapping fast, milliseconds instead of minutes. And it revealed that bootstrapping is programmable: internally it rotates an encrypted lookup table, so the same operation that refreshes noise can also evaluate a small function.

Yet circuits kept being evaluated the old way: one gate, one bootstrapping. A 64-bit operation compiles to thousands of gates, so it cost thousands of bootstrappings.

The primitive was powerful. The way circuits used it was not. That is the wall.

Make every bootstrapping count

If one bootstrapping can evaluate a small function, why spend it on a single AND? The right question is how to feed several encrypted bits in and read several results out, so that one bootstrapping swallows a whole cluster of gates.

The mathematical machinery took shape in the open literature. A 2018 result by Carpov, Izabachène and Mollimard [4] showed how to pack multiple encrypted inputs into one ciphertext through a weighted combination, and how to factor the lookup table so that a single rotation, the expensive part, yields several outputs at almost no extra cost.

We built on this foundation deliberately: every cryptographic building block in our stack comes from the open literature and from our own peer-reviewed research. A clean, independent lineage from top to bottom.

But a toolkit is not a technology. Researchers could hand-craft specific compound gates and show they worked. Nobody had the general theory: given an arbitrary piece of logic, how do you design the gate that computes it? That open problem is what our AutoHoG research [5] solves.

Gate design, automated

AutoHoG’s first contribution is a theory of homomorphic gate design. Hand it any small boolean function, multiple inputs, multiple outputs, and it derives the custom gate automatically: the input weights, the encrypted lookup table, and the guarantee that everything stays inside the noise budget. What used to be a cryptographer’s craft became a procedure a machine executes. In our toolchain, a solver does exactly that.

The second contribution scales this from one gate to entire circuits. AutoHoG takes standard Verilog, the same language chips are described in, runs it through industry-grade logic synthesis, then hunts through the circuit for subcircuits that can legally collapse into a single custom gate. It generates that gate on the fly, checks the constraints, and rewrites the netlist. No manual optimization anywhere in the loop.

The payoff, measured across adders, multipliers, and the standard ISCAS circuit benchmarks: up to 5.7x lower latency than gate-by-gate evaluation, before any hardware acceleration enters the picture.

A workload hardware loves

Here is what falls out of that compilation, and why it matters. Whatever the original circuit was, the entire computation collapses into one uniform primitive executed over and over: a weighted sum, then a bootstrapping. Same shape, same memory pattern, integer arithmetic, no data-dependent branching. Ask anyone who designs silicon: one primitive to accelerate means one pipeline to perfect.

On GPUs we pushed this to its conclusion with one deliberate choice: every polynomial operation runs in exact integer arithmetic using the number-theoretic transform. No floating-point FFT anywhere in the pipeline.

Why so strict? Floats round, and rounding drifts across hardware, so two machines can produce subtly different ciphertexts from identical inputs. In a decentralized network, that is fatal. With pure integer math, every node computes exactly the same bytes. We verified it the hard way: independent nodes, deliberately scheduled differently, reproduced byte-identical ciphertexts across our entire operator suite. Consensus-grade determinism, by construction.

The numbers

Measured on 8x NVIDIA RTX 5090:

  • One bootstrapping: ~5 ms
  • One confidential transfer (64-bit encrypted balances: balance check, subtract, add, select): ~90 ms
  • Throughput: 210+ confidential transfers per second
  • Scaling: 8 GPUs deliver 8.0x one GPU

And look at the hardware again: consumer GPUs, the same silicon sold to gamers, available off the shelf anywhere in the world. No datacenter-exclusive accelerators, no six-figure hardware budget. This is not an accident. Our pipeline is latency-bound and clock-hungry, and high clock speed is exactly where consumer GPUs shine. The economics of confidential computation change when anyone can rack the hardware.

From GPUs to silicon

The same regularity points forward. A workload that is one integer pipeline with static dataflow is exactly what FPGAs and ASICs are best at. GPUs are our today. Dedicated silicon, computing the exact same bytes, is the natural next step, and the scheme was shaped for that journey from day one.

Privacy that costs milliseconds stops being a feature you justify and becomes a default you switch on. Confidential onchain finance, running at full speed. That is what we are building at Primus.
